**Chapter 2**

**Anatomy of a Cartridge: Components, Bullet Types, and Pressure Ratings**

*[IMAGE: An exploded-view diagram of a complete handgun cartridge showing the four primary components — primer, case, powder charge, and bullet — separated and labeled, with arrows indicating how they assemble into a loaded round]*

Before diving into the deeper questions of terminal performance, you need a firm grasp on what ammunition actually is, how it is constructed, and how the various parts interact. This chapter walks through every major component of a handgun cartridge, surveys the full landscape of modern bullet designs, and explains the pressure ratings that govern what you can safely load in your firearm. By the end, you will be able to look at any defensive round on a store shelf and understand exactly what you are holding.

**Getting the Vocabulary Right**

Precision matters when discussing ammunition, and imprecise language leads to confusion. A **bullet** is only the projectile — the piece that leaves the barrel and travels downrange. It is not the entire loaded round. The complete assembly of primer, case, propellant, and bullet is called a **cartridge** or a **round**. "Shells" is a term properly reserved for shotgun ammunition and artillery ordnance; handgun ammunition consists of cartridges, not shells.

The brass container that holds everything together is the **case** (also referred to informally as brass or an empty, once fired). The explosive compound at the rear of the case is the **primer**, and the chemical propellant between the primer and the bullet is the **powder charge**.

**Caliber** refers to the nominal bore diameter, expressed in decimal inches in the American system or in millimeters in the metric system. However, the number stamped on the headstamp does not always correspond precisely to the actual bullet diameter. A 9mm Parabellum does use a bullet measuring approximately 9mm (.355 inch), but a .38 Special fires a bullet that is actually .357 to .358 inch across. A cartridge labeled ".32" can use bullets ranging from .308 to .321 inch depending on the specific cartridge in question. The lesson: always verify the exact specifications for your firearm rather than assuming the name tells the whole story.

A **cartridge** describes a loaded round of a specific caliber, and a specific handgun is chambered for a specific cartridge. A .38 Special revolver fires .38 Special ammunition — not .38 S&W, not .38 Super, not any other ".38" that happens to exist. Compatibility is determined by chamber dimensions, not by the number on the box.

> ***Key Takeaway: A bullet is only the projectile. The complete loaded round is a cartridge. Caliber designations do not always match actual bullet diameters, and cartridges are not interchangeable simply because they share a number in their name. Always verify that your ammunition matches your firearm's exact chambering.***

**Primers: The Ignition System**

The primer is a small cup of impact-sensitive chemical compound seated in the base of the cartridge case. When the firing pin or striker hits it, it detonates, producing a flash of hot gas that ignites the main powder charge.

Historically, the first primer compound was mercury fulminate. While effective, it came with serious drawbacks: its combustion residue attracted moisture (hygroscopic byproducts), and the mercury itself embrittled brass cases, making them unsuitable for reloading. During the black powder era, these problems were partially masked by the heavy residue that black powder itself produced. The transition to smokeless propellants, however, made mercury-based primers untenable.

The replacement compound that became the industry standard is **lead styphnate**. It produces no hygroscopic residue, contains no mercury, and is extraordinarily stable over time — properly stored ammunition with lead styphnate primers can remain reliable for decades.

The lead content in standard primers does release trace amounts of vaporized lead upon firing. For the vast majority of recreational shooters, this exposure is negligible. The concern becomes meaningful primarily in institutional settings: range officers and instructors who spend eight hours a day, five days a week on indoor firing lines can accumulate enough airborne lead to require medical monitoring. This institutional exposure is what drives the development of **lead-free priming compounds**, which eliminate both lead and barium from the ignition mixture.

Early generations of lead-free primers suffered from inconsistent shelf life, which gave rise to an enduring myth that the government was deliberately engineering primers to expire. In reality, those early formulations were simply immature technology rushed into service before long-term testing was complete. Modern lead-free primers have largely resolved these reliability issues, though ongoing durability testing continues.

**Propellant Powder: More Complex Than It Appears**

Smokeless propellant — commonly called "powder" — is the energy source that drives the bullet down the barrel. Different powder formulations burn at different rates, and each has an optimal pressure range where it burns most efficiently and cleanly. Below that range, combustion is incomplete, leaving excessive residue or even unburned granules. Above it, pressure behavior can become unpredictable.

Some propellants exhibit what engineers call **non-linear behavior** at elevated pressures: a modest increase in charge weight produces a disproportionately large spike in pressure. This is one of the reasons handloaders must follow published load data meticulously and why ammunition manufacturers conduct extensive pressure testing before releasing a product.

Two additional variables that manufacturers must consider are **density** and **flow**. A powder may have the ideal burn rate for a given cartridge, but if its granules are too bulky, there simply is not enough room inside the case to fit an adequate charge beneath a seated bullet. Conversely, a powder that is too fine or irregular in granule shape may not flow consistently through automated dispensing equipment on a high-speed production line, introducing unacceptable variation in charge weight.

The interplay of burn rate, density, flow characteristics, and case volume means that only a handful of powder formulations may be suitable for any given cartridge-and-bullet combination. When all variables align, the result is consistent, reliable ammunition. When they do not, the result is a load that is inaccurate, inconsistent, or potentially dangerous.

**Cartridge Cases: The Structural Foundation**

Brass cartridge cases are manufactured through a process called **deep drawing**. A flat disk — called a coin — is punched from a strip of gilding metal (an alloy of approximately 95% copper and 5% zinc). This disk is then progressively pressed through a series of forming dies, each one drawing it into a longer, thinner cylinder. Between draws, the metal is annealed (heated and slowly cooled) to restore its ductility, since repeated deformation hardens and embrittles brass.

The base of the case, including the rim and primer pocket, is intentionally left work-hardened so it can withstand the forces of firing. Case mouths and necks, which must grip the bullet and release it cleanly under pressure, receive a final anneal to keep them pliable.

Modern defensive ammunition frequently uses **nickel-plated cases**. The nickel coating reduces friction for smoother feeding and extraction, resists oxidation during long-term storage in a holster or magazine, and provides a visual distinction from standard range ammunition — a useful identification feature when you need to differentiate carry loads from practice rounds at a glance.

**Bullet Construction: A Complete Survey**

The bullet is where the most significant engineering advances have occurred, and understanding the different construction methods is essential to evaluating defensive ammunition.

**Lead Bullets**

Lead has been the primary bullet material for centuries, and for good reason: it is dense (708 pounds per cubic foot), has a low melting point (621 degrees Fahrenheit), is abundant, and is soft enough to be formed easily. Modern lead bullets are produced either by **casting** molten lead alloy into molds or by **swaging** — feeding sections of lead wire into a hydraulic press that compresses them into shape.

Swaged lead bullets are extremely uniform and inexpensive to produce, making them ideal for target and practice ammunition. However, pure lead is soft, which limits its performance at higher velocities (it can deposit material in the barrel, a condition called leading) and makes it a poor choice for defensive applications — soft lead deforms unpredictably on impact rather than expanding in a controlled manner.

Cast bullets can incorporate alloying elements — primarily **antimony** and **tin** — to increase hardness. Hard-cast bullets, particularly those with wide, flat nose profiles (large meplats), penetrate deeply in a straight line without deforming. This makes them excellent for certain hunting applications but generally unsuitable for personal defense, where controlled expansion and limited over-penetration are priorities.

**Jacketed Bullets (Cup-and-Core)**

The most common construction method for defensive ammunition wraps a lead core inside a copper-alloy jacket. The jacket is manufactured through deep drawing — similar to case production but on a smaller scale — creating a cup into which a lead core is inserted and hydraulically pressed. The jacket controls the bullet's behavior on impact: a **full metal jacket (FMJ)** has the open end at the base, enclosing the nose entirely, so the bullet does not expand. A **jacketed hollow-point (JHP)** places the open end at the front, exposing a cavity designed to initiate expansion.

In a standard cup-and-core bullet, only friction holds the core inside the jacket. This works well for FMJ designs, but hollow-point expansion can create enough force to separate the jacket from the core — a failure mode that reduces both penetration and wound effectiveness.

The evolution of handgun bullet design was slower than for rifles. At typical handgun velocities, early hollow-point designs often failed to expand reliably or expanded so aggressively that they lost structural integrity. It was not until the late 1980s that truly effective expanding handgun bullets began reaching the market.

**Bonded Bullets**

Bonded construction was developed to solve the core-jacket separation problem. In this process, the lead core is essentially soldered to the interior of the copper jacket using heat, creating a metallurgical bond that prevents the two components from separating even during aggressive expansion. Only soft, nearly pure lead alloys can be bonded in this manner, which keeps the core ductile enough for reliable expansion.

The additional manufacturing steps make bonded bullets more expensive, but the payoff is significant: they expand without shedding jacket material, maintain their weight through barriers, and score consistently well in standardized testing protocols.

**Plated Bullets**

Plating takes a different path to the same goal. Lead cores are first swaged to near-final shape, then placed in electroplating baths where copper is deposited directly onto the lead surface. The result is a bullet where the jacket and core are inherently bonded at the molecular level — the copper is electroplated to the lead and cannot be separated without melting.

Some manufacturers further process plated bullets by running them through additional swaging dies to work-harden the plating, punch hollow-point cavities into the nose, and bring the bullets to final dimensional specifications. This produces defensive bullets with all the benefits of bonding at a potentially lower cost than traditional jacketed-and-soldered bonded designs.

**Mechanically Locked Bullets**

An alternative bonding approach uses internal ribs and rings machined into the jacket interior. When a higher-antimony (harder) lead core is inserted and swaged into place, these mechanical features lock the core and jacket together without relying on soldering. This allows the use of a harder lead alloy — which holds together better when punching through intermediate barriers like automotive glass or sheet metal — while still preventing core-jacket separation.

**Coated Bullets**

Coated bullets apply a polymer or synthetic layer over a lead core to prevent bore leading without requiring a copper jacket. Early versions from the 1970s used nylon-based coatings and performed adequately for their era, though they could not withstand the rigors of modern barrier testing.

Contemporary coated bullets are designed primarily for training and competition. The polymer coating is applied just thickly enough to protect the bore, reducing both lead exposure and barrel fouling. For high-volume competitive shooters firing tens of thousands of rounds annually, even marginal cost savings per round translate to significant budget relief over a season.

**All-Copper Expanding Bullets**

Eliminating lead entirely, all-copper bullets are machined or formed from solid copper stock. The hollow-point cavity allows the petals to unfold on impact, producing expansion. Since there is no separate core, there is zero risk of core-jacket separation — the bullet is a single homogeneous piece of metal.

The trade-off is density. Copper is approximately 79% as dense as lead, so an all-copper bullet of the same external dimensions will weigh less. To match the weight of a comparable lead-core bullet, an all-copper design must be made longer — which creates challenges with cartridge overall length, case capacity, barrel twist rates, and bore friction (bearing surface). Manufacturers address the bearing-surface issue by cutting relief grooves into the bullet's body, reducing the area of metal-to-bore contact and keeping pressures within safe limits.

**Sintered and Frangible Bullets**

Sintered bullets are manufactured by compressing metal powder (usually copper, sometimes mixed with tin or a binding agent) under extreme hydraulic pressure. The resulting projectile holds together well enough to be loaded, fired, and to penetrate soft targets, but shatters into dust on contact with hard, smooth surfaces like steel plates.

This property makes frangible ammunition essential for close-range tactical training environments — shoot houses with steel walls, for instance — where conventional bullets would produce dangerous ricochets and fragmentation. However, frangible bullets are **not** designed for defensive use. Against soft tissue or ballistic gelatin, they typically behave similarly to full metal jacket rounds, penetrating without expanding in a controlled manner.

**Fluted Non-Expanding Designs**

A newer category of bullet uses aerodynamic flutes machined into its solid copper body instead of a hollow-point cavity. As the bullet enters a dense medium, the spinning flutes interact with the surrounding fluid (tissue, gelatin, water), creating lateral forces that rapidly decelerate the projectile and transfer energy to the target. The bullet stops at approximately the same depth as a conventional expanding hollow-point of the same caliber — but it does so without deforming.

This design offers exceptional barrier performance because the solid copper body passes through intermediate materials with virtually no alteration, then decelerates normally once it reaches tissue. Real-world terminal data on these designs is still accumulating, but controlled testing results have been noteworthy.

**Non-Hollow-Point Expanding Bullets**

In certain jurisdictions, hollow-point ammunition faces legal restrictions. Engineering solutions exist to work around this constraint: jacketed bullets with pre-scored internal stress lines and a compressible polymer insert in the nose. Upon impact, the polymer collapses, the pre-scored jacket opens outward along its engineered fracture lines, and the bullet increases its frontal diameter — achieving expansion without an exposed hollow-point cavity.

Because the polymer insert displaces some of the lead core, these bullets are lighter than a standard load of the same profile. Manufacturers compensate by loading to higher velocities, which is ultimately what drives the expansion mechanism.

> ***Key Takeaway: Modern defensive bullets fall into several distinct construction categories — cup-and-core, bonded, plated, mechanically locked, all-copper, and engineered non-hollow-point designs. Each solves a specific engineering problem. The most critical distinction for defensive use is whether a bullet can expand reliably while retaining enough mass to penetrate adequately — and modern bonded, plated-bonded, and all-copper designs are engineered specifically to do both.***

**Why Contemporary Bullets Outperform Their Predecessors**

Three engineering advances account for the dramatic improvement in modern defensive bullet performance.

First, the adoption of a **standardized testing protocol** gave manufacturers a common benchmark against which to measure their products. Before such a standard existed, each company was essentially guessing at what customers wanted. With a shared yardstick — even an imperfect one — the industry could focus resources on measurable improvement.

Second, manufacturers refined their control over **lead alloy composition and jacket metallurgy**. Selecting the right combination of core hardness, jacket thickness, and heat treatment allows engineers to tune a bullet's expansion characteristics for a specific velocity window and intended application.

Third, and most significantly, the introduction of **pre-scored and pre-notched jackets** transformed expansion reliability. An un-scored hollow-point jacket must tear at random weak points when expansion begins, which produces inconsistent mushroom shapes and unpredictable weight retention. Pre-cutting stress lines into the jacket nose creates engineered fracture points that open symmetrically, producing uniform expansion and predictable terminal behavior.

The combination of these three advances is why a quality defensive hollow-point manufactured today will outperform even the best designs available a generation ago by a wide margin.

**Understanding Pressure: MAP, +P, and +P+**

Every commercially manufactured cartridge operates within pressure limits defined by **SAAMI** (the Sporting Arms and Ammunition Manufacturers' Institute). The key specification is the **Maximum Average Pressure (MAP)** — the highest average chamber pressure permitted for a given cartridge. Manufacturers test each production lot to ensure their ammunition stays within this boundary.

Statistical controls add an additional layer of safety. The **Maximum Probable Lot Mean (MPLM)** is defined as two standard deviations above the MAP. A production lot must demonstrate at least a 97.5% probability that its mean pressure will not exceed the MPLM. If testing reveals the lot exceeds this threshold, the ammunition is rejected and either reworked or destroyed.

**+P ammunition** is loaded to a higher MAP than standard pressure for the same cartridge. SAAMI defines specific +P pressure limits for cartridges where it is permitted. For example, standard .38 Special operates at a MAP of 17,000 PSI; .38 Special +P is rated for 21,000 PSI. This additional pressure yields 100 to 200 feet per second of extra velocity, depending on barrel length and bullet weight — enough to meaningfully improve expansion and terminal performance, but at the cost of increased recoil, muzzle blast, and accelerated wear on the firearm.

Not every firearm is rated for +P ammunition. Before loading +P cartridges, verify that your specific handgun is designed and rated to handle the additional pressure. Using +P ammunition in a firearm not built for it can shorten the gun's service life or, in extreme cases, cause a dangerous failure.

**+P+ ammunition** occupies an unofficial category. There is **no SAAMI standard** defining +P+ pressure limits. These loads are produced by major manufacturers exclusively for law enforcement agencies that accept the associated liability. The requesting agency specifies a desired velocity, the manufacturer develops a load to meet it, and the department assumes responsibility for any consequences — reduced weapon lifespan, harsher recoil, accelerated case wear.

The velocity gain of +P+ over standard +P is typically modest — 50 to 100 additional feet per second. And pushing a bullet beyond its designed velocity window can actually degrade performance: a projectile that expands optimally at +P speeds may over-expand or fragment at +P+ velocities, resulting in insufficient penetration. More is not always better.

**Matching Bullets to Cartridges and Applications**

Every bullet is engineered to perform within a specific velocity range and for a specific purpose. A defensive hollow-point designed to expand reliably when fired from a 9mm Parabellum at 1,150 feet per second will almost certainly fail to expand when loaded into a lower-pressure .380 Auto cartridge that drives it at only 900 feet per second — there simply is not enough velocity to initiate the expansion mechanism. Conversely, that same .380-weight bullet loaded to 9mm velocities may exceed its structural limits, fragmenting into pieces rather than expanding in a controlled mushroom.

Similarly, a heavy hard-cast flat-nose bullet designed to penetrate three feet of large game animal will pass completely through a human-sized target with minimal energy transfer — it was never designed to stop in that amount of tissue. And a finely tuned defensive hollow-point that expands perfectly in gelatin testing will flatten against the shoulder bone of a large game animal without penetrating to vital organs.

The principle is straightforward: ammunition must be matched to the cartridge, the firearm, the expected velocity, and the intended application. There is no universal bullet. Understanding the engineering trade-offs covered in this chapter puts you in a position to evaluate any defensive load on its actual merits rather than on marketing claims alone.
