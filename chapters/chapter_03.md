**Chapter 3**

**The Myth of Stopping Power — And What Actually Matters**

*[IMAGE: A high-speed photograph capturing three different defensive handgun bullets at the moment of impact in calibrated ballistic gelatin — one fragmenting, one mushrooming uniformly, and one barely deforming — illustrating that identical-looking cartridges can produce wildly different terminal outcomes]*

Ask a room full of concealed carriers what they want from their ammunition and someone will use the phrase within thirty seconds: "stopping power." It is one of the most emotionally loaded terms in the firearms world, and also one of the most misleading. People talk about it as though it were a measurable property — a single number stamped on the box, like muzzle velocity or bullet weight. It is not. Stopping power, as popularly understood, is a fiction. A comforting abstraction. An attempt to reduce the chaotic, unpredictable reality of a lethal encounter into something simple enough to fit on an ammunition label.

That does not mean terminal performance is irrelevant. It absolutely matters. But the way most people frame the conversation — "which caliber has the best stopping power?" — reveals a fundamental misunderstanding of how human beings respond to gunshot wounds, how firearms are actually used in defensive encounters, and what variables genuinely influence the outcome. This chapter dismantles the myth, examines what the real-world data actually shows, and introduces the legal and physiological frameworks that should be shaping your thinking instead.

Before we go any further, a necessary framing: this entire discussion concerns scenarios involving an imminent, lethal threat to your life or the lives of others. Every concept here applies exclusively to situations where deadly force is legally and morally justified. Disagreements at family gatherings, road rage incidents that haven't escalated beyond shouting, confrontations that remain verbal — none of these are our topic. We are talking about the narrow, grave circumstances that satisfy the legal threshold for a deadly force response. Nothing else.

**What People Mean When They Say "Stopping Power"**

When most armed citizens invoke the concept, they are imagining a specific outcome: you fire your weapon at an attacker, and the attacker immediately ceases all hostile action. Collapses. Drops. Stops cold. The fantasy is instantaneous physical incapacitation from a single handgun round — a kind of biological off-switch activated by the right caliber-and-bullet combination.

This expectation is not rooted in physiology. It comes from decades of Hollywood gunfight choreography, anecdotal war stories retold and distorted through multiple generations, and an understandable human desire for certainty in terrifying situations. We want to believe that if we choose the right equipment, we can guarantee the right outcome. The universe does not work that way, and the human body is far more resilient than most people appreciate.

Here is a perspective that clarifies the problem: there are documented accounts of combatants absorbing rifle-caliber fire — rounds with several times the energy of any handgun cartridge — and continuing to fight. If projectiles delivering two thousand or three thousand foot-pounds of energy do not reliably produce instant incapacitation, the notion that your compact nine-millimeter or snub-nosed revolver possesses a magical "stopping" quality is not just optimistic. It is detached from reality.

**The Numbers That Reframe Everything**

The most effective antidote to stopping-power mythology is statistical perspective. Consider a representative sample of one thousand lawful defensive encounters involving armed citizens. The data from multiple compiled sources consistently reveals a pattern that should reshape how you think about ammunition selection.

Of those one thousand incidents, somewhere between five hundred and nine hundred are resolved without a single shot being fired. The mere visible presence of a firearm — drawn or displayed — causes the threat to disengage. The exact ratio is debated and difficult to pin down precisely, in part because many of these incidents go unreported. Armed citizens who successfully deter a threat by showing a weapon sometimes hesitate to involve law enforcement, concerned about the legal complexities surrounding the display of a firearm even in legitimate self-defense contexts.

From the remaining incidents where the threat persists, roughly half require the defender to actually handle the weapon in a ready posture but still do not result in shots fired. Of the encounters where shots are fired, a significant majority involve both parties missing entirely. Marksmanship under extreme stress, in poor lighting, with an elevated heart rate and degraded fine motor control, is a dramatically different skill from punching paper at a well-lit indoor range.

Among those who are actually struck by gunfire, the majority — roughly sixty percent — disengage immediately regardless of where they were hit or what caliber hit them. A minor wound to an extremity. A graze. Any contact at all. The psychological shock of being shot is, for most aggressors, sufficient to end their willingness to continue. This is a mental response, not a physical one. The bullet did not destroy their capacity to fight; it destroyed their desire to fight.

Another subset absorbs more significant wounds and still flees, requiring medical attention but retaining enough physical function to leave the scene. Only a small fraction — perhaps ten out of the original thousand — continue fighting after being hit. These are the cases where ammunition performance, shot placement, and caliber selection genuinely influence the outcome. Ten out of a thousand.

> ***Key Takeaway: In roughly ninety-nine percent of lawful defensive encounters, caliber and bullet design play little or no role in the outcome. The encounter is resolved by deterrence, by misses, or by the psychological impact of being struck — none of which depend on terminal ballistic performance. The fraction of incidents where ammunition selection is the decisive variable is vanishingly small. Prepare for it, but keep it in perspective.***

**Understanding Use-of-Force Law: The Foundation You Cannot Skip**

Before discussing how to optimize your ammunition for that rare worst-case encounter, you need to understand the legal architecture governing when you are permitted to use deadly force at all. Misunderstanding these principles can transform a justifiable defensive shooting into a criminal prosecution. The legal consequences of ignorance here are not fines or penalties — they are felony charges and prison sentences.

The standard framework for evaluating a civilian's use of lethal force involves four interconnected elements. Terminology and emphasis vary by jurisdiction, but the underlying logic is consistent across most of the United States.

**The Ability Element**

The first question any legal analysis will ask: did the aggressor possess the physical capability to inflict death or serious bodily harm? A person brandishing a firearm obviously satisfies this criterion. A person wielding a large edged weapon, a bludgeon, or an incendiary device likely does as well.

The gray areas are where careful judgment becomes critical. A large, physically powerful individual may represent a lethal threat even without a weapon — particularly against a smaller, older, or physically disadvantaged defender. Multiple unarmed assailants acting in concert can satisfy the ability requirement through what the legal system recognizes as disparity of force. Two against one is concerning. Three against one is compelling. But these assessments are intensely fact-specific, and the threshold varies by state statute and case law.

One critical nuance: ability must be *apparent* to the defender at the moment force is used. An attacker carrying a concealed weapon possesses the physical ability to kill, but if you have no reason to know that weapon exists, you cannot cite it to justify your actions. Your legal justification rests on what you reasonably perceived, not on what a post-incident investigation later revealed.

**The Opportunity Element**

Ability alone is insufficient. The attacker must also have the practical opportunity to employ that ability against you. A person holding a baseball bat on the far side of a six-lane highway during rush-hour traffic has the ability to cause harm but lacks the immediate opportunity to do so. That same person, thirty seconds later, sprinting across a gap in traffic toward you — the calculus has changed entirely.

Opportunity is dynamic. It can materialize in seconds and evaporate just as quickly. Distance, physical barriers, the nature of the threat (contact weapon versus ranged weapon), and the environment all factor into whether opportunity exists at any given instant.

**The Jeopardy and Intent Element**

Has the attacker demonstrated, through words or actions, an intention to use their ability and opportunity to harm you? A person walking purposefully toward you with a weapon in hand, making eye contact, in a context where violence is a reasonable expectation — that constellation of behaviors can communicate intent without a single spoken word.

Intent without opportunity is not enough. Opportunity without demonstrated intent is not enough. The elements work in combination, and each must be present simultaneously. Someone standing motionless beside a weapon while making verbal threats creates a different legal picture than someone advancing rapidly toward you with that weapon raised.

**The Preclusion Element**

In many jurisdictions, deadly force is lawful only when no reasonable alternative exists. Could you have retreated safely? Could you have escaped through an available exit? Could you have avoided the confrontation entirely by leaving the area earlier?

The rise of stand-your-ground statutes and castle doctrine laws has modified this requirement in many states, particularly for encounters inside your own home or vehicle. In those locations, many jurisdictions have eliminated the duty to retreat entirely. But not all have, and the specific rules vary substantially. Knowing the precise legal landscape in your state is not optional — it is a prerequisite to carrying a firearm responsibly.

Consult a qualified attorney who specializes in self-defense law in your jurisdiction. General internet research is a starting point, not a substitute for professional legal guidance. Many concealed carry training programs include legal instruction, and attending one taught by a credentialed legal expert is among the most valuable investments you can make.

**The Law Enforcement Distinction**

A common source of confusion is the belief that concealed carriers and law enforcement officers operate under identical rules. They do not. The legal frameworks overlap in places, but the obligations and authorities differ in important ways.

A sworn officer responding to a violent crime is generally expected to pursue, engage, and resolve the threat. Retreat is typically not an option for on-duty law enforcement. An armed citizen, in contrast, is generally afforded — and in some jurisdictions required to exercise — the option to disengage and seek safety whenever that option exists. The officer has a duty to confront; you may have a duty to avoid.

This distinction matters for our ammunition discussion because it shapes the kinds of encounters you should be preparing for. A law enforcement officer may need to fire through intermediate barriers, engage at extended distances, or sustain a prolonged exchange of fire. Your defensive scenario, statistically, involves close range, a few seconds of total duration, and limited rounds fired. The ammunition that is ideal for one context is not necessarily ideal for the other.

**How Incapacitation Actually Works**

With the legal framework established, we can turn to the physiology that the stopping-power myth obscures. When a defensive round strikes a human body, there are precisely two mechanisms by which it can produce rapid physical incapacitation — and everything else is either slow, unreliable, or both.

The first mechanism is disruption of the central nervous system. A projectile that damages the brain or the upper spinal cord can produce immediate loss of motor function. This is the only mechanism that reliably causes instantaneous incapacitation. It is also, from a marksmanship standpoint, extraordinarily difficult to achieve under combat conditions. The target area is small, the encounter is dynamic, and fine motor control under extreme stress is severely degraded.

The second mechanism is rapid blood pressure loss through hemorrhage. A bullet that damages major blood vessels or highly vascular organs (the heart, the liver, the major arteries) causes blood loss that eventually drops pressure below the level required to sustain consciousness. The critical word is *eventually*. Even catastrophic cardiovascular damage does not produce instantaneous incapacitation. A person whose heart has been destroyed may retain enough oxygenated blood in their brain to continue conscious, directed action for ten to fifteen seconds. In a gunfight, fifteen seconds is an eternity.

Everything else — pain, fear, peripheral tissue damage, minor blood loss — contributes to the psychological dimension of the encounter but does not reliably produce physical incapacitation. A determined, motivated, chemically impaired, or psychologically committed attacker may continue fighting through wounds that would cause an ordinary person to collapse. This is the biological reality that stopping-power mythology refuses to acknowledge.

> ***Key Takeaway: There are only two reliable mechanisms for physically stopping a human attacker with a handgun: central nervous system disruption (instantaneous but requires extremely precise shot placement) and rapid hemorrhagic blood pressure loss (effective but not instantaneous — a fatally wounded person can remain dangerous for critical seconds). Everything else depends on the attacker's psychological willingness to stop, which no ammunition can guarantee.***

**The Mental Dimension of Combat**

Understanding the psychological component is essential because it governs the outcome far more often than the physical component. In the vast majority of defensive encounters, what stops the attacker is not tissue destruction — it is a decision. The decision to stop. The decision that continuing is not worth the risk. The decision that being shot, or being shot at, changes the calculation beyond the point of willingness.

This psychological dimension works in both directions. A defender who is mentally prepared — who has rehearsed scenarios, understood the legal framework, developed confidence through training, and accepted the gravity of lethal force before the moment arrives — will perform dramatically better than one who is encountering these realities for the first time in the middle of a crisis. Mental readiness does not replace marksmanship, but it enables it. Without it, even excellent shooting skills may never be deployed.

Prior conditioning shapes expectations, and expectations shape behavior under extreme stress. An attacker who has been shot and *expects* to be incapacitated may comply with that expectation — falling, stopping, retreating — even if the wound itself is survivable and non-disabling. Conversely, an attacker who is mentally committed to continuing the fight, or whose neurochemistry is altered by substances or adrenaline, may absorb objectively serious wounds and keep pressing the attack.

This is why you will find accounts of identical cartridges producing wildly different outcomes in different encounters. The variable is not the ammunition. The variable is the person it hit.

**Applying Force: Continuous, Proportionate, and Decisive**

Popular media has conditioned people to expect gunfights to unfold in neat, deliberate pairs of shots followed by pauses for assessment. Competitive shooting sports reinforce this pattern. Reality does not cooperate.

In an actual defensive encounter, the evidence overwhelmingly shows that defenders fire continuously until one of several conditions occurs: the threat ceases, the attacker disappears from view, the defender's ammunition is exhausted, or the defender is incapacitated. There is no tactical pause to evaluate the effect of each round. The neurocognitive state of a person fighting for their life does not permit that kind of calm, sequential decision-making.

This means two things for your ammunition planning. First, you will almost certainly fire more rounds than you expect. The typical defender, in post-incident interviews, cannot accurately recall how many times they pressed the trigger. Second, the cumulative effect of multiple well-placed rounds matters far more than the theoretical performance of any single projectile. A magazine of reliably expanding, adequately penetrating defensive rounds, delivered to the target with reasonable accuracy, is a far better plan than a single magic bullet that you expect to end the encounter on its own.

Legal awareness is critical here as well. You are authorized to apply force as long as the lethal threat persists — and you are required to stop the instant it does not. The boundary between justified and unjustified use of force is defined by the moment the threat ends. Continuing to fire after the attacker is down, fleeing, or otherwise no longer a threat converts a lawful act of self-defense into potential criminal conduct. Understanding this in advance — not discovering it afterward — is part of your obligation as an armed citizen.

**Why Shot Placement Dominates Every Other Variable**

Every serious discussion of terminal performance eventually arrives at the same conclusion: where a bullet strikes matters far more than what bullet strikes. This is the single most important lesson in the entire stopping-power debate, and it cannot be overstated.

A premium, technologically advanced hollow-point that hits an attacker in the forearm will produce a survivable, non-incapacitating wound. A basic, inexpensive round-nose projectile that strikes the brainstem will produce immediate incapacitation. The first round may be objectively "better" ammunition. The second round produced the better outcome. The difference is anatomy, not engineering.

This does not mean bullet design is irrelevant — we will spend considerable time in later chapters examining why some projectiles perform better than others in tissue. But it does mean that the hierarchy of priorities for a defensive shooter should be clear: the ability to place rounds accurately under stress comes first. Ammunition selection is a secondary optimization applied on top of a marksmanship foundation.

The practical implication is straightforward. Time and money spent on advanced shooting courses, realistic stress-inoculation training, drawing and presenting under pressure, and developing reliable accuracy at close-quarters defensive distances will deliver far greater returns than any amount of ammunition comparison shopping. Choose a quality defensive load from a reputable manufacturer, verify that it functions reliably in your specific firearm, and then pour your remaining resources into the skill that makes that ammunition effective: putting it where it needs to go.

**Why Stopping Power "Formulas" Always Fail**

Over the decades, numerous attempts have been made to reduce terminal effectiveness to a single numerical index. These formulas typically combine some mixture of bullet diameter, weight, velocity, and construction into a score that purports to rank cartridges by their ability to incapacitate. Some of these models have been influential. None of them have been definitive.

The fundamental problem is reductive reasoning. Human physiology is not a fixed target. The same bullet, at the same velocity, striking the same anatomical region, can produce dramatically different outcomes depending on the individual's body composition, blood alcohol level, adrenaline state, pain tolerance, psychological commitment, and even the angle of impact. A predictive model that ignores all of these variables — and every formula does, because they cannot be known in advance — is inherently incomplete.

What these formulas can tell you is whether a given load falls within the broad envelope of adequate performance: sufficient penetration to reach vital structures, sufficient expansion to maximize the wound channel, sufficient weight retention to maintain penetration through intermediate barriers. These are useful parameters. But treating a numerical stopping-power index as a meaningful predictor of real-world outcomes is confusing precision with accuracy. The number is precise. The prediction is not accurate.

The honest conclusion, drawn from decades of accumulated data, shooting reviews, and medical examinations, is this: among modern, quality defensive handgun loads from any of the major calibers, the differences in real-world effectiveness are far smaller than most people want to believe. A nine-millimeter hollow-point, a forty-caliber hollow-point, and a forty-five-caliber hollow-point, all from reputable manufacturers and all loaded with modern projectile technology, will produce broadly similar outcomes when placed in the same anatomical location. The caliber wars that consume so much energy in online forums are arguments about margins that rarely determine real-world results.

The variables that genuinely determine outcomes — shot placement, number of effective hits, the defender's composure and decisiveness, the attacker's psychology and physiology — are not printed on any ammunition box. Accept this, and you are already ahead of most armed citizens in your understanding of what defensive ammunition can and cannot do.
