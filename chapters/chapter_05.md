**Chapter 5**

**A Century of Stopping Power Formulas — And Why None of Them Settled the Debate**

*[IMAGE: A timeline-style illustration spanning from the early 1900s to the present day, showing silhouettes of key handgun types from each era (revolver, 1911, wonder-nine, modern polymer-frame) alongside stylized bar charts representing different stopping-power ranking systems — conveying that the quest for a definitive formula has persisted for over a hundred years without resolution]*

The previous chapters established that stopping power, as a single measurable attribute, does not exist in any meaningful scientific sense. Yet for more than a century, researchers, military officers, law enforcement analysts, and dedicated hobbyists have tried to create exactly that — a numerical formula or scale that distills the terminal effectiveness of a cartridge into a single figure, allowing side-by-side comparison. The appeal is obvious. If you could look at a chart and say, "This load scores 87 and that one scores 64, so the first one is better," the entire question of ammunition selection would be resolved in minutes.

None of them succeeded. But you need to understand why — and what each attempt got right and wrong — because these theories are still cited constantly in gun shops, online forums, training classes, and marketing materials. If you do not know the history, you have no defense against people presenting decades-old, flawed conclusions as established fact.

**The Pre-Smokeless Baseline: When Bigger Was the Only Option**

Before smokeless powder changed everything in the 1890s, terminal ballistic "theory" was brutally simple: if you wanted more effect on target, you made the projectile larger. Black powder, due to the way it combusts, has a practical velocity ceiling of roughly 1,400 feet per second. No matter how much powder you packed behind the bullet, you could not push it significantly faster. This meant the only available variable for increasing lethality was mass and diameter.

For centuries, this was the operating principle of every military and civilian arm. Muskets in the eighteenth century were essentially smoothbore weapons firing lead spheres of .70 to .75 caliber — roughly equivalent in bore diameter to a modern 12-gauge shotgun. Rifles offered improved accuracy at the cost of dramatically slower loading times, and their calibers, while smaller, were still substantial by modern standards — a "small bore" military rifle might be .40 or .45 caliber. By the mid-nineteenth century, rifled muskets in the .50 to .58 caliber range dominated, still using black powder and still limited by the same velocity ceiling.

Within this framework, there was no need for complex formulas. A .45 hit harder than a .36, which hit harder than a .32. The relationship was intuitive and roughly linear. Then smokeless powder arrived and shattered that simplicity overnight.

**The Smokeless Velocity Revolution**

The introduction of smokeless propellant in the 1880s and 1890s changed the fundamental equation of terminal ballistics. Rifle cartridges leaped from black-powder velocities of 1,200 to 1,400 feet per second to smokeless velocities of 2,000 feet per second and beyond. Within two decades, further refinements pushed service rifle velocities above 2,700 feet per second. The tactical implications were enormous — flatter trajectories, greater effective range, reduced need for precise range estimation.

But handguns did not share in this windfall, and the reason is important to understand. The primary constraint is chamber pressure. Black powder cartridges generate maximum pressures in the range of 14,000 to 15,000 pounds per square inch. Smokeless rifle cartridges operate at 40,000 to 60,000 PSI — pressures that can be safely contained within eight or more pounds of steel and wood in a full-sized rifle action. A handgun, whose entire reason for existing is compactness and portability, cannot be built to those specifications without becoming impractically heavy.

Improvements in metallurgy and firearm design eventually allowed handgun pressures to rise into the mid-30,000 PSI range for the hottest standard cartridges. But the gains in velocity were modest compared to rifles. A 9mm pushing a 125-grain bullet at 1,150 feet per second, boosted to maximum pressure, might gain another 200 feet per second — meaningful, but not transformative. A .45 ACP loaded to +P pressures can push a 230-grain projectile from 850 to perhaps 1,000 or 1,100 feet per second, at which point felt recoil becomes punishing for most shooters and the platform becomes difficult to control.

The result: handguns, even with modern smokeless propellants, remain fundamentally low-powered compared to rifles and shotguns. They exist in a narrow performance window where improvements are measured in increments, not leaps. This explains the extraordinary intensity of the stopping-power debate. When your tool operates near the margins of effectiveness, squeezing out every possible advantage feels critically important — even if the measurable differences between options are small.

**The Stockyard Experiments: Where It All Began**

The first systematic attempt to compare handgun cartridge effectiveness took place in 1904, when two military officers — one from the infantry branch and one from the medical corps — visited a Chicago slaughterhouse with an assortment of service handguns. Their mission: determine whether the newer high-velocity, small-bore, full-metal-jacketed cartridges emerging from European arsenals were as effective at incapacitation as the older, larger-caliber lead projectiles the American military had traditionally relied upon.

The testing protocol was, by any modern standard, deeply crude. Cattle were selected at random and shot through the lungs with various calibers and bullet types. The animals were then observed for their response. If an animal did not expire promptly, it was dispatched by slaughterhouse workers using standard methods. Some animals received multiple hits; shot placement and wound tracks varied uncontrollably between subjects. On a second day of testing, the experimenters fired as rapidly as possible — up to ten rounds per animal — recording how quickly each went down. A third component involved firing into suspended cadavers and measuring the physical displacement of limbs and torso upon impact.

Critically, the animals were not dissected after testing to examine the internal wound tracks. They were simply sent into the processing line. The cadavers were examined more carefully, with wound tracks studied and recorded. But the live-animal testing — which was the emotional and persuasive centerpiece of the entire project — produced no internal wound data whatsoever.

By modern scientific standards, the study lacked controls, standardized protocols, and reproducible methodology. But it produced the conclusion that the military establishment wanted: the .45 caliber was demonstrably superior to smaller-bore alternatives. That conclusion shaped American military handgun doctrine for the next century, and its influence persists today — long after the methodology that produced it has been recognized as inadequate.

**The First Mathematical Scale: Sectional Area Times Energy**

In the 1930s, a prominent military ordnance expert took the stockyard data and attempted to formalize it into a mathematical ranking system. The formula was straightforward: multiply the bullet's cross-sectional area (in square inches) by its muzzle energy (in foot-pounds), then apply a shape correction factor to account for bullet profile.

By this calculation, a standard 9mm Parabellum load earned a score of roughly 30. A .45 ACP scored approximately 49. A .380 Auto came in around 14. The numbers were clean, simple, and satisfyingly precise.

The problem becomes apparent immediately: does a .45 ACP genuinely deliver twice the incapacitation effect of a 9mm? Is it truly three and a half times more effective than a .380? No credible body of real-world data supports ratios that extreme. The scale conflated measurable physical quantities (area, energy) with incapacitation outcomes in a way that produced numbers that looked authoritative but did not correspond to observed reality.

Additional inconsistencies undermined the formula's internal logic. The shape factor — a subjective multiplier assigned to account for bullet profile — varied between loadings in ways that were difficult to justify. Why would one full-metal-jacket bullet profile receive a different shape correction than another of similar geometry in a different caliber? These assignments were estimates built on estimates, producing a framework that appeared mathematical but rested on subjective foundations.

For decades, the formula's prestige — bolstered by the reputation of its creator — insulated it from serious challenge. It was not until modern ballistic testing methods became available that its predictions could be checked against controlled data. They did not hold up.

> ***Key Takeaway: The appeal of a simple numerical formula is powerful, but the earliest stopping-power scales were built on crude testing, subjective correction factors, and assumptions about the relationship between physical measurements and incapacitation outcomes that real-world data does not support. A number that looks precise is not the same as a number that is accurate.***

**The Big-Game Hunter's Index**

After the Second World War, an entirely different approach emerged from the African hunting world. A legendary professional hunter, drawing on decades of experience shooting large and dangerous game across the continent, developed a knock-out index designed to rank cartridges by their ability to anchor heavy animals — elephants, buffalo, and the like.

The formula incorporated bullet weight, velocity, and caliber into a single comparative number. For its intended purpose — selecting between large-bore rifle cartridges for use on thick-skinned game with non-expanding solid bullets — the index had some practical utility. Experienced hunters found that its rankings roughly matched their field observations.

The problem arises when this index is stretched beyond its original context. The formula was built around rifle cartridges. It was validated against large animal physiology. It assumed non-expanding projectiles and deliberate, aimed shots taken before the target was aware of the hunter's presence — the opposite of a defensive handgun scenario. When applied to handgun cartridges, expanding bullets, close-range encounters, and human physiology, the index produces rankings that have no meaningful connection to observed outcomes.

Yet it continues to appear in ammunition discussions, cited by people who may not realize that its foundational assumptions have nothing in common with the defensive handgun problem.

**The Computer Simulation Disaster**

In 1975, a major government research institute published what should have been a landmark study. It employed modern technology — high-speed photography, computer modeling, and a three-dimensional anatomical simulation of the human body — to produce a comprehensive, data-driven ranking of cartridge effectiveness. The ambition was admirable. The execution was catastrophically flawed.

The first error was methodological: the researchers used ballistic gelatin mixed to a twenty-percent concentration rather than the standard ten percent. The denser gelatin distorted the results, exaggerating the temporary cavity produced by high-velocity loads and misrepresenting the actual tissue response at handgun velocities.

The second error was conceptual: the study measured the **temporary** wound cavity and treated its entire volume as "destroyed" tissue. As we established in the previous chapter, the temporary cavity in handgun wound ballistics is largely irrelevant — human tissue is elastic enough to absorb that displacement and return to function. Only the permanent cavity represents actual tissue destruction. By scoring the temporary cavity as meaningful damage, the study systematically overvalued high-velocity, light-bullet loadings that produce impressive but physiologically inconsequential temporary cavities.

The third error compounded the first two. Inexperienced shooters fired single shots at silhouette targets under time pressure, and the hit distributions were recorded for each caliber. These distributions were then overlaid on a computer model of the human body that had been divided into fifteen thousand small cubes, each assigned a lethality value by a panel of physicians. The computer calculated a composite score by multiplying the probability of hitting each cube by the volume of tissue "destroyed" at that location.

The predictable result: soft-recoiling, high-velocity loads scored highest, because their lighter recoil produced more center-mass hits from untrained shooters, and their inflated temporary cavities registered as greater tissue destruction. By this methodology, a 9mm full-metal-jacket load scored higher than a .45 ACP — a conclusion that contradicts virtually every other body of evidence on the subject.

The fundamental flaw was conflating two separate questions — "how likely is a hit?" and "how effective is a hit?" — into a single score, then distorting the effectiveness measurement by relying on the wrong wound channel. The study demonstrated that less recoil improves hit probability for untrained shooters, which is true and useful. But it told us nothing reliable about the terminal performance of the rounds tested.

**The Secret Animal Tests**

In the early 1990s, reports emerged of an elaborate series of animal tests conducted in secret at an undisclosed location. According to published summaries, several hundred domestic goats of uniform body weight were each fitted with surgically implanted arterial pressure sensors, connected to neurological monitoring equipment, positioned in a controlled stall, and shot with various handgun loads. Their physiological responses were recorded, autopsies were performed, and the data was compiled into an average incapacitation time for each cartridge tested.

The results, as published, suggested that velocity mattered but that the returns diminished beyond a certain threshold — the additional recoil and muzzle blast of extremely high-velocity loads did not produce proportionally better incapacitation times.

The problem: the raw data was never released. The experimenters were never publicly identified. The testing facility was never disclosed. No independent researcher has ever been able to examine the underlying numbers, replicate the methodology, or verify the conclusions. This violates the most basic requirement of scientific credibility — that data and methods be available for independent review.

Some industry insiders have privately expressed the belief that the tests did occur. But without verifiable data, the results cannot be treated as anything more than an interesting but unconfirmed anecdote. They cannot form the basis for ammunition selection decisions.

**The Street-Data Approach**

Perhaps the most ambitious attempt to measure stopping power empirically came from a pair of law enforcement professionals who spent years collecting real-world shooting reports from police departments across the country. Rather than testing in a laboratory or simulating on a computer, they attempted to build a database of actual shootings and derive effectiveness ratings from observed outcomes.

The concept was sound: if you could accumulate enough shooting incidents, record the cartridge used, the shot placement, and the outcome, you could eventually construct a statistical picture of which loads produced faster incapacitation. It was the closest anyone had come to measuring stopping power as it actually manifests in the real world.

The execution, however, encountered insurmountable obstacles. First, the data was inherently uncontrolled. Unlike a laboratory experiment where variables can be isolated, every real-world shooting involves dozens of uncontrollable factors — the assailant's body composition, intoxication level, psychological state, the exact angle and depth of the wound, the defender's accuracy, the distance, the lighting. Parsing meaningful signal from this volume of noise requires enormous sample sizes and sophisticated statistical controls.

Second, the raw data had to remain confidential. The shooting reports were obtained from law enforcement sources who could not afford to have closed cases reopened or relitigated because details appeared in a published analysis. This meant no independent researcher could examine the underlying data, check the classifications, or verify the conclusions — the same transparency problem that plagued the secret animal tests.

Third, to manage the complexity, the researchers limited their analysis to single-shot, torso-hit incidents. While understandable as a simplification, this decision excluded a vast amount of potentially informative data: multi-hit encounters, extremity hits, incidents where a second shot followed so quickly that the first shot's effect could not be isolated. The resulting dataset, while large, was not representative of the full spectrum of defensive shootings.

The published results ranked cartridges and specific loads by their one-shot-stop percentage, carried to fractional precision. Critics noted that the confidence intervals around these percentages — given the quality and limitations of the underlying data — should have been far wider than the presented rankings implied. The data was, in statistical terms, noisier than the conclusions suggested. Clean-looking results derived from messy data raise legitimate questions about methodology.

The effort advanced the field by demonstrating that empirical street data could be collected and analyzed. But the specific rankings it produced are best understood as rough approximations with wide uncertainty bands, not as precise performance differentials between loads.

> ***Key Takeaway: Every major attempt to reduce stopping power to a formula or ranking has failed — not because the researchers were incompetent, but because the problem is fundamentally irreducible. Human physiology is too variable, real-world encounters are too chaotic, and the number of uncontrollable factors is too large for any single number to be predictive. What the accumulated century of effort does tell us is this: among modern defensive handgun calibers loaded with quality expanding ammunition, the performance differences are far smaller than any of the formulas suggest. Your training, shot placement, and composure will determine the outcome far more than any choice between credible defensive cartridges.***

**What a Century of Failure Actually Teaches Us**

The history of stopping-power formulas is not a story of steady progress toward a correct answer. It is a story of repeatedly discovering the same uncomfortable truth from different angles: handguns are marginal tools for incapacitation, the variables that determine outcomes are mostly uncontrollable, and the measurable differences between competent defensive loads are small.

This does not mean ammunition selection is irrelevant. A quality expanding hollow-point in a proven defensive caliber is meaningfully better than ball ammunition or an underloaded cartridge. The distinction between adequate and inadequate ammunition is real and important. But the distinction between one adequate load and another adequate load — the space where the stopping-power debate lives — is measured in fractions of a percentage point, buried under the far larger variables of shot placement, number of hits, attacker psychology, and the defender's own preparedness.

If you take one lesson from this history, let it be this: no formula will save you. No chart will give you certainty. The most productive use of the time and mental energy that people pour into stopping-power debates is to redirect it toward training, practice, legal education, and situational awareness — the variables you can actually control.
