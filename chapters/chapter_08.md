**Chapter 8**

**The FBI Testing Protocol: Eight Tests That Redefined Ammunition Standards**

*[IMAGE: An overhead photograph of a complete FBI-protocol testing setup — eight gelatin blocks arranged in a row, each with its corresponding barrier material (bare, heavy clothing layers, steel sheets, wallboard panels, plywood, and angled auto glass) positioned in front, with labeled cards identifying each test — conveying the scope and rigor of the full evaluation procedure]*

The previous chapter traced how institutional failure and three decades of caliber evolution produced the most widely adopted ammunition evaluation standard in the world. This chapter breaks that standard apart — test by test, measurement by measurement — so you understand exactly what the protocol evaluates, what each test represents in real-world terms, and how to interpret the results when comparing defensive loads for your own use.

Before examining the procedure itself, an important distinction needs to be established. The FBI protocol was designed to evaluate ammunition for **law enforcement use**. The operational demands of a sworn officer and a concealed-carrying civilian are not identical, and the protocol's emphasis on barrier penetration reflects the specific challenges law enforcement faces. Understanding where your needs overlap with — and diverge from — those of a federal agent will help you decide which elements of the protocol are critical for your ammunition selection and which are informative but not essential.

**Law Enforcement vs. Civilian Operational Context**

A law enforcement officer responding to a violent crime is generally expected to remain on scene, engage the threat, and resolve the situation. Retreat is rarely an option. This operational requirement means officers frequently find themselves engaging threats that are positioned behind, beside, or inside vehicles; sheltering behind building materials; or using furniture and other objects as improvised cover.

Officers in urban environments may need to fire through an automobile door to stop a suspect using the vehicle as cover — or through a vehicle windshield when a suspect attempts to use the vehicle itself as a weapon. They may need to fire through interior walls when a barricaded suspect is concealed within a structure. Every one of these scenarios involves intermediate barriers between the muzzle and the target.

Your situation as a civilian concealed carrier is fundamentally different. You are not required — and in most jurisdictions are strongly discouraged — from pressing an engagement or pursuing a fleeing threat. Flight is not just acceptable; it may be legally required. Your most likely defensive scenario involves close range, minimal or no intermediate barriers, and a few seconds of total engagement time. The probability that you will need to fire through a car door or a sheet of auto glass is not zero, but it is substantially lower than for an officer whose job requires vehicular pursuits and high-risk traffic stops.

This means the barrier tests in the FBI protocol are valuable information, but they need not be the deciding factor in your ammunition selection. A load that performs excellently in bare gelatin and through heavy clothing but is mediocre through auto glass may be a perfectly rational choice for a civilian carrier — especially if the trade-off buys you reduced recoil, better accuracy, or higher confidence in your ability to place shots under stress.

**The Protocol: Structure and Scale**

The full FBI ammunition evaluation protocol requires **eight separate gelatin tests** per load, each using five shots per test — for a total of forty gelatin blocks consumed per ammunition type evaluated. Additionally, twenty rounds are fired through a test barrel and twenty through a service weapon to establish velocity data, and two ten-shot groups are fired through each barrel to assess accuracy. The material and labor investment is enormous, which is why complete FBI-protocol testing is expensive and not conducted casually.

All gelatin blocks conform to the standardized preparation process covered in the previous chapter: ten-percent concentration, cooled to 39 degrees Fahrenheit, and calibrated with a BB-gun verification shot before use. Handgun blocks measure six by six by sixteen inches.

For the first six tests, the gelatin block is positioned with its front face ten feet from the muzzle of the test weapon. Tests seven and eight extend this distance to twenty yards. Each test fires one round per block, centered on the front face and aligned parallel to the sides. After each shot, penetration depth is measured to the nearest quarter inch, and the recovered bullet is extracted, weighed, and measured for expanded diameter.

The five recovered bullets from each test are averaged to produce mean penetration depth, mean expanded diameter, and mean weight retention for that test condition.

**Test One: Bare Gelatin**

The simplest test and the baseline against which all barrier tests are compared. The round is fired directly into the calibrated gelatin block with nothing between the muzzle and the block face except ten feet of air.

This test establishes the maximum performance potential of the bullet in an unobstructed soft-tissue simulant. It represents the scenario of an encounter with a lightly dressed or undressed individual in warm weather — minimal clothing, no barriers, no obstructions.

If a bullet designed to expand fails to perform properly in bare gelatin, there is no reason to proceed with barrier testing. This is the entry-level qualification: if it does not work here, it will not work anywhere.

**Test Two: Heavy Clothing**

The gelatin block's front face is covered with a standardized layering of four fabric types: a cotton undershirt material, a cotton dress shirt material, a down-insulated layer with a cambric shell, and a layer of heavy cotton denim. These layers are not stitched or quilted together — they are loosely draped to simulate the way a person wearing winter work clothes would actually present layers of fabric over their torso.

This test addresses one of the most significant practical vulnerabilities of hollow-point ammunition: clothing obstruction. When a hollow-point bullet strikes fabric before entering tissue, the cavity in the bullet's nose can capture a plug of cloth material. This plug fills the expansion cavity and prevents the hydraulic forces in tissue from initiating expansion. The result: a hollow-point that behaves like a full metal jacket round — sailing straight through the target without expanding, overpenetrating, and failing to create an adequate permanent wound channel.

The heavy-clothing test was one of the earliest and most important discoveries of standardized gelatin testing. Bullets that expanded beautifully in bare gel would, when the same block was covered in cloth, pass completely through without opening up. This finding drove the development of modern bullet designs engineered to resist clogging — polymer-tipped cavities, scored jacket petals, and mechanical bonding techniques that ensure expansion occurs even when the cavity contains debris.

**Test Three: Steel Barrier**

Two six-inch-square sheets of 20-gauge, hot-rolled, galvanized steel are positioned three inches apart, simulating the two steel skins of an automobile door panel. The steel barrier is placed ten feet from the muzzle, with the gelatin block positioned eighteen inches behind it. A single layer of light fabric (the cotton undershirt material) is draped over the gelatin's front face to simulate a shirt worn against the skin.

This test evaluates a bullet's ability to retain sufficient mass, velocity, and structural integrity to penetrate adequately and expand after passing through the lightest section of an automobile door — the portion containing only the outer and inner sheet metal skins with an air gap between them. The thicker sections of a car door — containing window mechanisms, impact reinforcement bars, and electric motors — will stop virtually any handgun bullet and are not represented in this test. Defeating those structures requires rifle-caliber projectiles.

**Test Four: Wallboard Barrier**

Two six-inch-square pieces of standard half-inch gypsum drywall are placed three-and-a-half inches apart, eighteen inches in front of the gelatin block. This simulates a standard interior wall in a wood-frame residential or commercial building — two sheets of drywall separated by the depth of a wall stud cavity.

This test is directly relevant to both law enforcement and civilian contexts. A home defender engaging an intruder who has taken cover behind an interior wall needs to know whether their ammunition will penetrate the wall and still perform effectively on the other side. Equally important: a defender needs to understand that virtually every defensive handgun bullet will pass through standard interior drywall. If family members are in rooms behind the threat, this is a critical factor in shot placement decisions.

**Test Five: Plywood Barrier**

A single six-inch-square piece of three-quarter-inch AA fir plywood is placed ten feet from the muzzle, with the gelatin block eighteen inches behind. This test represents solid wooden barriers such as exterior doors, furniture, and non-structural wooden panels.

Plywood is denser and harder than gypsum drywall, presenting a more demanding barrier to bullet penetration. A bullet that performs well through wallboard may deform prematurely or shed jacket material when encountering plywood, reducing its ability to expand and penetrate to adequate depth in the gelatin behind it.

**Test Six: Automobile Glass at Close Range**

A fifteen-by-eighteen-inch piece of quarter-inch laminated automobile safety glass is positioned at a 45-degree backward angle (simulating a windshield rake) and rotated 15 degrees laterally to create an offset impact angle. The glass is ten feet from the muzzle, gelatin eighteen inches behind, with light fabric draped over the gel face.

Laminated automobile glass is one of the most punishing barriers a handgun bullet can encounter. Unlike tempered side glass (which shatters into small fragments on impact), laminated windshield glass consists of two layers of glass bonded to a tough plastic interlayer. The glass cracks and fragments on impact, but the plastic layer holds the assembly together. This combination strips jacket material, deforms bullet noses, and can dramatically redirect a projectile's path.

The angled presentation compounds the challenge. A bullet striking glass at an angle tends to deflect and deform asymmetrically, which can cause it to tumble or veer off its original trajectory after passing through. Testing at a compound angle evaluates the worst-case deflection scenario.

**Test Seven: Heavy Clothing at Twenty Yards**

Identical to Test Two, but with the distance from muzzle to barrier extended from ten feet to twenty yards. This test evaluates the effect of velocity loss over distance on the bullet's ability to expand through clothing.

In practice, the velocity reduction over seventeen additional yards is modest for most defensive handgun cartridges — often less than the velocity loss caused by switching from a full-size duty barrel to a compact concealed-carry barrel. This test generally confirms rather than contradicts the results of Test Two, but provides data for the less common scenario of a defensive engagement at extended range.

**Test Eight: Automobile Glass at Twenty Yards**

Identical to Test Six, but at twenty yards and with only the 45-degree backward tilt — the 15-degree lateral rotation is removed. This represents a more straightforward angle of engagement with a vehicle windshield at a distance where the bullet has lost some velocity.

> ***Key Takeaway: The eight FBI protocol tests evaluate ammunition performance across a systematic range of realistic barrier scenarios — from the simplest (bare gelatin) to the most demanding (automobile glass at compound angles). For a civilian concealed carrier, the bare gelatin and heavy-clothing tests are the most directly relevant. The barrier tests (steel, wallboard, plywood, auto glass) are valuable supplementary data but reflect law enforcement operational needs that may not match your most likely defensive scenario. Weight the results accordingly.***

**How Scoring Works**

The FBI scores tested ammunition against clearly defined thresholds:

**Penetration:** The bullet must reach a minimum of twelve inches and must not exceed eighteen inches in calibrated gelatin. A bullet that penetrates eleven and three-quarters inches — even if it expanded to twice its original diameter — fails. A bullet that passes through eighteen inches — even if it expanded and retained all its weight — also fails. The twelve-to-eighteen-inch window is non-negotiable.

**Expansion:** Greater expansion earns a higher score. The measurement taken is the maximum expanded diameter — the widest point across the recovered bullet, not an average. Expansion is valuable because a wider bullet sweeps a wider permanent wound channel, increasing the probability of intersecting vital structures.

**Weight Retention:** Bullets that hold together score higher than bullets that fragment or shed their jackets. A bullet that separates into core and jacket fragments loses mass along the wound track — mass that may or may not contribute to useful tissue disruption. More importantly, a fragmenting bullet often fails to penetrate to adequate depth, as the lighter individual fragments experience more drag and stop sooner than an intact projectile would.

**Barrier Performance:** The ability to expand and penetrate adequately after passing through the test barriers is evaluated. A bullet described as "barrier blind" is one that performs consistently regardless of which barrier it encounters — maintaining expansion and penetration within the acceptable window whether it passed through bare gel, heavy clothing, steel, wallboard, plywood, or auto glass.

**The Velocity Factor and Caliber Implications**

The physics of the FBI protocol inherently reward velocity. Penetrating barriers requires energy. Expanding after barrier transit requires the bullet to retain enough velocity for the hydraulic forces in gelatin to initiate deformation. Achieving twelve inches of penetration through demanding barriers requires momentum that lighter, faster bullets can sometimes deliver more efficiently than heavier, slower ones.

This creates a measurable advantage for cartridges that generate higher velocities — the 9mm Parabellum, the .357 Magnum, and the .357 SIG all benefit from their velocity profiles in the barrier tests. The .45 ACP and .44 Special, which achieve their terminal effects primarily through mass and diameter at lower velocities, may score less impressively on barrier tests despite being thoroughly effective in unobstructed tissue.

The velocity advantage also means that barrel length matters. A nine-millimeter load that passes all eight FBI tests from a full-size duty pistol with a four-and-a-half-inch barrel may struggle to meet penetration minimums when fired from a subcompact concealed-carry pistol with a three-inch barrel. The shorter barrel produces lower velocity, which translates directly to reduced penetration and expansion. If you carry a compact or subcompact firearm, verify that your chosen ammunition has been tested from a barrel length comparable to yours — not just from a full-size duty weapon.

Similarly, traditional large-bore cartridges at standard pressures face challenges. The classic revolver loads that earned formidable reputations in previous generations — heavy lead bullets at moderate velocities — may fail the FBI barrier tests not because they are ineffective, but because the test rewards the specific combination of velocity and modern bullet engineering that those older designs were not built to deliver. Modern loadings in these calibers typically use lighter, faster hollow-point bullets specifically engineered to pass the protocol — a design shift driven entirely by the testing standard.

**The Protocol Is Not a Theory**

This distinction is worth emphasizing one final time: the FBI ammunition testing protocol does not predict outcomes. It does not claim that a bullet scoring 95 will stop an attacker and a bullet scoring 80 will not. It does not rank cartridges on a scale of "stopping power" or assign probabilities of incapacitation.

What it does is measure observable, quantifiable bullet performance in a standardized medium under controlled conditions. The underlying logic is a chain of reasonable assumptions:

- Ballistic gelatin is a consistent, validated simulant of human soft tissue.
- Bullets that penetrate to adequate depth are more likely to reach vital structures.
- Bullets that expand create wider permanent wound channels.
- Bullets that hold together penetrate more reliably than bullets that fragment.
- Bullets that perform consistently through barriers are more versatile than bullets that do not.

Each link in this chain is rational and supported by decades of testing. But the chain does not extend to a prediction about what will happen in your specific encounter with your specific firearm against your specific threat. It gives you the best available basis for making an informed choice — and that is all any testing protocol can honestly offer.

**Do You Need Full FBI-Compliant Ammunition?**

The honest answer: not necessarily. The FBI protocol evaluates ammunition for the needs of federal law enforcement agents — professionals who may face barrier-rich environments, extended engagements, and scenarios that require shooting through vehicles and structures. Your needs may be different.

If your primary concern is a close-range defensive encounter against a lightly clothed attacker in a residential or urban environment, a load that excels in bare gelatin and heavy-clothing testing but is mediocre through auto glass may serve you perfectly well. If reduced recoil improves your accuracy and confidence — which, as we have established, matters far more than marginal differences in terminal performance — a load that falls slightly below the FBI penetration threshold may be a rational choice for your specific circumstances.

Several ammunition lines are designed specifically for this market: loads optimized for reduced recoil, manageable muzzle blast, and reliable performance in the most common civilian defensive scenarios — without attempting to achieve full FBI barrier-protocol compliance. These are not inferior products. They are purpose-built solutions for a different operational context.

The armed citizen who dismisses any ammunition that does not achieve a perfect FBI score is making the same error as the person who obsesses over stopping-power formulas: mistaking a testing benchmark for a guarantee of outcomes. Choose ammunition that performs reliably in your firearm, that you can shoot accurately and confidently, and that meets the penetration and expansion standards appropriate to your most likely defensive scenario. The FBI protocol is a valuable reference tool. It is not the only consideration.

> ***Key Takeaway: The FBI protocol is the gold standard for law enforcement ammunition evaluation, but it was designed for law enforcement operational needs. As a civilian carrier, prioritize bare-gelatin and heavy-clothing performance — the tests most relevant to your likely scenarios. Use barrier test results as supplementary information, not as pass-fail requirements. And never sacrifice accuracy, controllability, or confidence for marginal gains in barrier penetration that you are unlikely to need.***
