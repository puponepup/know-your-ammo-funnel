**Chapter 9**

**Budget Testing Alternatives: What Works When Gelatin Is Too Expensive**

*[IMAGE: A comparison photograph showing three testing setups side by side — a row of water-filled plastic jugs, a wooden trough filled with water-saturated newspaper pulp, and a clear synthetic gelatin block — illustrating the spectrum of testing options from cheapest to most accurate]*

The previous chapters established that standardized ballistic gelatin testing is the gold standard for evaluating defensive ammunition. It is reproducible, validated against tissue response, and universally accepted. It is also expensive, labor-intensive, and logistically demanding. A full FBI-protocol test for a single load consumes forty gelatin blocks, requires nearly half a ton of prepared material, and demands refrigeration equipment and careful temperature control throughout the process.

For the individual shooter who wants to understand how a specific load performs but cannot justify the cost and complexity of formal gelatin testing, alternatives exist. None of them replicate the precision and reproducibility of calibrated gelatin, but several can provide useful comparative data at a fraction of the cost. This chapter examines what is available, what each method tells you, and — critically — what limitations you must account for when interpreting results from these substitutes.

**The Historical Context: What People Shot Before Gelatin**

Before standardized testing protocols existed, ammunition writers and experimenters demonstrated bullet performance by shooting whatever was inexpensive and visually dramatic. Bars of soap were popular for small-caliber testing — a hollow-point fired into a bar of hand soap produced an impressive cavity that made for good photography. The problem: soap is an inelastic medium, and what it shows is the temporary cavity frozen in place — which, as we have established, is largely irrelevant to handgun terminal performance.

Aerosol cans, paint cans, and soda cans all made for spectacular images when they exploded mid-flight after being struck by a bullet. The visual drama was undeniable. The correlation to tissue damage was zero. These demonstrations proved exactly nothing about how a bullet would perform in a defensive encounter, but they sold magazines and generated enthusiasm.

The transition to more credible testing media began when researchers started using materials that approximated the density and response characteristics of muscle tissue. Water-saturated sawdust was popular in the Pacific Northwest where sawmill waste was readily available. Bundles of water-soaked newsprint served a similar purpose. Both approaches worked reasonably well for evaluating rifle ammunition intended for large game, but their application to handgun ammunition was limited by inconsistency and difficulty in measurement.

The modern budget alternatives fall into three categories: water-based containers, bulk media (wet paper), and synthetic gelatin substitutes. Each has specific advantages and specific limitations.

**Water-Filled Container Testing**

The simplest and least expensive testing approach uses water as the bullet-stopping medium, contained in disposable plastic jugs. The classic method involves lining up one-gallon milk or water jugs in a row on a table and firing through them, counting how many the bullet penetrates and recovering the projectile from the final jug or the area beyond.

The cost is minimal. Empty milk jugs are free if you have access to a milk-drinking household and can accumulate rinsed empties over time. Filling them with tap water costs pennies. A five-shot test series might use twenty jugs — forty dollars if you purchase pre-filled water jugs, but negligible cost if you use saved empties and municipal water. Cleanup involves collecting the punctured plastic for recycling and letting the spilled water drain. There is no smell, no rotting organic material, and minimal environmental impact.

A more refined variant places one-gallon zip-seal bags filled with water into a wooden trough. This setup — sometimes called a water-bag box — allows more precise measurement of penetration distance, as you can mark the trough interior and measure where the bullet stopped relative to the trough's starting point. The plastic bags are thinner and more uniform than the walls of milk jugs, reducing the variability introduced by the container itself.

Both approaches share the same fundamental advantages: low cost, simple setup, minimal cleanup, and results that are reasonably correlated with gelatin performance. But they also share significant limitations that you must account for when interpreting results.

**Understanding the Correlation Problem**

The most commonly cited rule of thumb for converting water penetration to gelatin-equivalent depth is to divide the water penetration distance by 1.55. By this formula, a bullet that travels twenty-four inches through water jugs would be expected to penetrate approximately fifteen and a half inches in calibrated gelatin.

This correction factor works reasonably well for many projectiles. But extensive testing by independent researchers has revealed a critical limitation: the correlation is not universal. Some bullets — with no obvious pattern to predict which ones — require dramatically different correction factors. Bullets have been documented that require divisors as high as 2.1 or 2.2 to accurately match their water penetration to their observed gelatin performance.

This variability means you cannot reliably use water-jug penetration data to make precise predictions about how a specific load will perform in gelatin. You can use water testing to compare multiple loads against each other — firing them all through the same jug setup and observing which penetrates deeper — but translating that comparative ranking into absolute gelatin penetration numbers introduces uncertainty that cannot be eliminated.

A second problem is that water jugs are harder on bullets than bare gelatin. The relatively tough plastic of the jug walls acts as an abrasive medium, applying mechanical stress to the bullet as it passes through each container. Bullets that retain jacket-to-core integrity in gelatin may separate in water jugs. This makes water-jug testing harsher than bare-gelatin testing but less demanding than the FBI barrier protocols. It occupies a middle ground between best-case and worst-case scenarios.

**Practical Considerations for Water Testing**

If you decide to pursue water-jug testing, several practical details matter.

First, the plastic jugs are fragile. High-density polyethylene milk jugs are manufactured to the thinnest wall thickness that can reliably hold liquid — approximately two hundredths of an inch. They are blow-molded in a process that leaves stress points, and those stress points become failure points over time. If you store filled jugs for later use, the stressed containers will crack and leak. Cold storage accelerates this degradation — thermal cycling causes the plastic to become brittle and split. Use stressed jugs first and store only undamaged ones.

Second, water is incompressible. When a high-velocity bullet strikes a water-filled container, the hydraulic shock propagates through the liquid instantly. The jugs will rupture violently, spraying water in all directions. You will get wet. Your firearm will get wet. Your shooting bench and everything on it will get wet. Within a few test shots, you will be standing in a shallow pool of muddy water. Dress appropriately — waterproof boots, quick-drying clothing, and protective covers for electronic equipment.

Third, if you build a wooden trough for water-bag testing, engineer it to withstand hydraulic shock. The water bags will jet liquid upward and out the entrance hole when struck. The force can split poorly constructed boxes or cause joints to fail. Reinforce corners, use waterproof sealant on seams, and do not assume that a lightweight frame will survive repeated impacts from magnum-caliber projectiles.

**Wet Paper and Bulk Media**

An alternative approach uses densely packed, water-saturated paper or sawdust pulp as the stopping medium. This method was more popular decades ago when bundled newspapers and thick telephone directories were readily available at curbside on recycling day. Today, the declining circulation of print newspapers and the near-disappearance of paper phone books make acquiring sufficient bulk paper challenging.

If you can source the material, the procedure involves soaking newsprint or shredded paper in water until it reaches a saturated, pulpy consistency, then packing it tightly into a container or directly into piles on the shooting bench. Alternatively, a wooden trough can be filled with the wet pulp and fired into directly.

The method has advantages for evaluating rifle ammunition intended for large game, as the dense, fibrous medium approximates the resistance of heavy muscle and connective tissue. But for handgun testing, the approach introduces significant problems.

First, the medium is difficult to standardize. How wet is "saturated"? How tightly should the paper be packed? Different degrees of compression produce different stopping characteristics, and there is no simple calibration method comparable to the BB-gun test used for gelatin. Results are inherently inconsistent from one test session to another.

Second, the material is heavy. A gallon of water weighs approximately eight pounds. A carton of five thousand sheets of copy paper weighs sixty pounds dry. A trough filled with twenty gallons of water-saturated newsprint can easily exceed three hundred pounds. The support structure must be robust enough to handle this mass without shifting when struck by bullet impact.

Third, recovering bullets and measuring penetration is labor-intensive. You must dig through soggy, compacted pulp to locate each projectile. Finding jacket fragments or pieces of a bullet that separated is nearly impossible. Measuring exact penetration depth through non-uniform, compressed paper is imprecise at best.

For these reasons, wet-paper testing is uncommon among serious handgun experimenters today. It remains occasionally useful for rifle testing in rural areas where bulk sawdust or mill pulp is readily available, but for defensive handgun evaluation, water jugs or synthetic gelatin substitutes are more practical.

> ***Key Takeaway: Water-filled jugs offer the lowest-cost testing option with reasonably useful comparative results, but the penetration-to-gelatin correlation is inconsistent and cannot be relied upon for precise predictions. Wet paper and bulk media are labor-intensive, difficult to standardize, and better suited to rifle testing than handgun evaluation. For informal testing, water jugs work well for side-by-side load comparisons. For anything approaching scientific rigor, synthetic gelatin substitutes are a better investment.***

**Synthetic Gelatin Alternatives**

The third category of budget alternatives uses synthetic compounds formulated to approximate the ballistic response of animal-derived gelatin without requiring refrigeration, elaborate preparation, or biological decomposition management.

Several proprietary formulations are commercially available. These products share common advantages: they are non-biological (so they do not rot, attract insects, or produce odors), they are optically clear (making bullet and wound-track photography substantially easier), they can be stored indefinitely at room temperature, and they can be re-used multiple times by melting and re-casting.

The typical synthetic block arrives pre-cast and ready for use. You remove it from the shipping container, position it on your shooting bench, and fire. No mixing, no cooling, no BB-gun calibration. After firing, you recover the bullet, note the penetration depth and wound-track dimensions, and either discard the block or save it for re-use.

Re-using a synthetic block is straightforward but requires patience. Remove any bullet fragments still embedded in the material — anything left behind will cloud the block and make photography difficult in subsequent tests. Place the block in its original mold (or chop it into smaller pieces to fit), then heat it in an oven at approximately 250 to 270 degrees Fahrenheit for several hours until the material fully liquefies and all trapped air bubbles rise to the surface and escape. Allow the mold to cool slowly for at least twelve hours before removing the re-cast block.

A critical safety note: do not attempt to accelerate cooling by submerging the hot mold in a water bath. The mold will be at 250+ degrees. Water boils at 212 degrees. The result is violent steam generation, which can cause burns, splashing, or loss of the molten material into the cooling water. Be patient and let it cool naturally.

**How Closely Do Synthetics Approximate Real Gelatin?**

The formulation of synthetic gel products is typically proprietary — manufacturers do not publish the exact chemical composition or mixing ratios. Material safety data sheets describe the contents in broad terms (mineral oils, polymer gelling agents) but classify the specifics as trade secrets. This is sound business strategy but makes independent verification of performance claims difficult.

What matters for practical purposes is whether synthetic gel produces results that correlate well with animal-derived ballistic gelatin. Independent testing by ammunition manufacturers and research facilities suggests that well-formulated synthetic products come reasonably close. Penetration depths are generally within an inch or two of what the same load produces in calibrated animal gelatin. Expansion diameters are comparable. Wound-track morphology is similar enough for visual comparison.

The synthetic products are not identical to animal gelatin — but animal gelatin itself is a simulant, not a perfect replica of human tissue. The synthetic gels are a simulant of a simulant, one additional step removed from the original target. For budget-conscious experimenters who want better data than water jugs provide but cannot justify the cost and complexity of traditional gelatin, the synthetic alternatives represent a reasonable compromise.

**Limitations and Honest Expectations**

No budget testing method replicates the precision and reproducibility of FBI-protocol testing with calibrated animal gelatin. Water jugs give you rough comparative data with unpredictable correlation to gelatin penetration. Wet paper is labor-intensive and inconsistent. Synthetic gels are better but still one step removed from the validated standard.

If you are an ammunition manufacturer preparing a product for law enforcement evaluation, you must use real gelatin tested under FBI protocols. If you are a defensive shooter trying to decide between three loads for your carry gun, shooting them through water jugs or synthetic gel will give you enough information to make an informed choice — as long as you understand the limitations and do not treat the numbers as absolutes.

Use budget testing for what it is: a screening tool, a comparison method, and a way to gain hands-on understanding of how bullets behave in a soft medium. Do not mistake it for the kind of rigorous data that comes from standardized laboratory testing. And always cross-reference your informal results against published gelatin data from credible sources when it is available.

> ***Key Takeaway: Budget testing alternatives are useful tools for informal comparison and hands-on learning, but they are not substitutes for standardized gelatin testing when precision matters. Water jugs work well for rough side-by-side load comparisons despite inconsistent correlation to gelatin. Synthetic gel products offer better accuracy and convenience at moderate cost. Choose your testing method based on your specific needs — informal comparison versus absolute performance measurement — and interpret results accordingly.***
