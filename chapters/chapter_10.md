**Chapter 10**

**Practical Testing: Equipment, Documentation, and Methodology**

*[IMAGE: An organized ballistic testing workspace showing a chronograph, digital calipers, electronic scale, waterproof notebook, specimen boxes with numbered compartments, and recovered test bullets arranged in sequence — illustrating the essential equipment and documentation system for conducting credible ammunition testing]*

The previous chapter covered the various media you can use to test ammunition performance at different cost points — from water jugs to synthetic gelatin to calibrated animal-derived gel. But having access to a testing medium is only the first step. Conducting tests that produce useful, credible data requires systematic methodology, meticulous record-keeping, and an understanding of what variables matter and how to control them. This chapter walks through the practical steps of setting up, executing, and documenting ammunition tests in a way that generates information you can trust and compare across multiple test sessions.

**The Equipment You Actually Need**

Serious testing requires more than just ammunition and something to shoot it into. Whether you are conducting informal water-jug tests or attempting to replicate FBI protocols with synthetic gel, certain tools are non-negotiable if you want your results to be more than casual observation.

**A chronograph** is mandatory. Velocity is the single most important variable that changes when you switch from one barrel length to another, one ammunition lot to another, or one environmental condition to another. Without documented velocity data, you cannot interpret your penetration and expansion results in any meaningful context. Modern chronographs are inexpensive — quality units are available for under a hundred dollars. Position the chronograph according to the manufacturer's instructions (typically ten to fifteen feet from the muzzle), fire your test rounds through it, and record every velocity reading. Calculate the average, note the extreme spread (the difference between fastest and slowest), and document the temperature and atmospheric conditions at the time of testing.

**Digital calipers** are essential for measuring expanded bullet diameter. Dial or vernier calipers work, but digital units provide instant readouts with no interpretation required. Quality calipers are available for twenty to forty dollars. Measure the widest dimension across the expanded bullet — not an average of multiple measurements, but the single maximum diameter. This is the standard measurement convention used by ammunition manufacturers and testing laboratories.

**An electronic scale** allows you to weigh recovered bullets and calculate weight retention. If you reload, you already own one. If not, digital powder scales suitable for bullet weighing are available in the same price range as calipers. Weigh the unfired bullet from the same lot (or use the published bullet weight if you are confident the lot matches specification), weigh the recovered bullet, and calculate the percentage of original weight retained. Bullets that fragment or shed jacket material will show significant weight loss.

**A waterproof notebook** or weatherproof data sheets are necessary because testing involves water, mud, and general outdoor messiness. Standard paper notebooks become illegible when soaked. Waterproof field notebooks designed for outdoor use cost a few dollars more but remain usable in wet conditions.

**Storage containers** for recovered bullets prevent damage and cross-contamination. Small compartmentalized boxes — the kind used for organizing hardware or fishing tackle — work well. Number each compartment and match it to your written records. Do not toss recovered bullets into a single container where they will ding each other and lose the fine detail that makes visual comparison useful.

**What to Record: The Non-Negotiable Data Points**

If you do not write it down at the time of testing, you will not remember it accurately later. Memory is unreliable even for single tests. Once you have conducted a dozen tests across multiple sessions, keeping the details straight without written records becomes impossible.

For every test shot, document the following minimum data:

**Date and time.** Environmental conditions change throughout the day and across seasons. Temperature affects both ammunition performance and gel consistency (if using synthetic products that claim temperature stability, verify it).

**Location.** If you test at multiple ranges or locations, note where each test occurred. Indoor versus outdoor, enclosed bay versus open range — these details matter if you later notice patterns in your data.

**Firearm used.** Model, barrel length, and any relevant modifications. The same ammunition fired from a four-inch barrel and a three-inch barrel will produce different velocities and potentially different terminal performance.

**Ammunition specifics.** Manufacturer, product line, bullet weight, advertised velocity, and lot number. The lot number is stamped on the box flap and identifies the specific production run. Ammunition performance can vary slightly between lots, and if you later observe unexpected results, the lot number allows you to cross-reference against other documented tests or contact the manufacturer if a quality issue is suspected.

**Distance to target.** Ten feet is standard for most tests, but if you are replicating specific FBI protocol tests, some are conducted at twenty yards. Record the actual measured distance, not your estimate.

**Testing medium.** Specify exactly what you shot into — calibrated gelatin at 39 degrees, synthetic gel at room temperature, water jugs (how many and what size), wet paper (how prepared and how compressed). The more specific you are, the more useful the data becomes for comparison.

**Velocity readings.** All of them, not just the average. Record every shot across the chronograph. Note the ambient temperature when velocity was measured.

**Penetration depth.** Measured to the nearest quarter inch for gelatin, to the nearest jug for water testing. Describe the stopping point — did the bullet stop inside the final medium, exit the back, lodge between containers?

**Expanded diameter.** Maximum measurement across the widest point of the recovered bullet.

**Recovered bullet weight.** Compare to unfired bullet weight to calculate retention percentage.

**Visual observations.** Did the jacket separate from the core? Did petals fold back symmetrically or asymmetrically? Did the bullet fragment? Did any part of the bullet fail to perform as designed? Photographs are valuable here, but written notes capture details that images sometimes miss.

**Impressions and anomalies.** Anything unusual, unexpected, or worth remembering for future reference. If the third shot behaved differently from the first two, note it. If you changed a variable mid-session (switched ammunition, adjusted distance, experienced equipment malfunction), document it.

> ***Key Takeaway: Disciplined record-keeping transforms casual shooting into credible testing. Record every relevant detail at the time of testing — date, location, firearm, ammunition lot, distance, medium, velocity, penetration, expansion, weight retention, and observations. Without complete documentation, your test results are anecdotes, not data. Invest in basic measurement tools (chronograph, calipers, scale) and systematic documentation, and your testing will produce information that retains value for years.***

**Organizing and Preserving Recovered Bullets**

Recovered test bullets are physical evidence of performance. They allow visual comparison between loads, provide data for measurements you may not have taken initially, and serve as reference specimens if you later acquire better photography equipment or want to re-examine details. But only if you preserve them properly and keep them organized.

Assign each recovered bullet a unique identifier that corresponds to your written records. A simple numbering system works — Test 001, Test 002, and so on. Write the identifier on a small label and place it in the storage compartment with the bullet, or if space is tight, mark the compartment itself.

Store bullets in individual compartments to prevent contact damage. Expanded petals are fragile. If bullets rub against each other in a shared container, edges get bent, petals break off, and fine detail is lost. The effort you invested in testing is wasted if your specimens are damaged before you can examine them.

For presentation or teaching purposes, a common method is to mount recovered bullets on labeled cardboard or foam-core boards. Glue or tape each bullet in place, write the test details directly on the board beside it, and you have a self-documenting display that allows side-by-side visual comparison without needing to cross-reference a separate notebook. This approach is particularly effective for law enforcement training where multiple students need to examine the same specimens.

Professional testing laboratories photograph each recovered bullet, measure and record all data, then store the bullets in compartmentalized specimen boxes filed by test number. The photographs and written records go into three-ring binders organized by caliber and test date. This level of organization is not excessive — it is the minimum standard required when testing is conducted for product development or regulatory submission.

**Replicating Standard Protocols — Or Documenting Your Deviations**

If you are testing ammunition using water jugs, wet paper, or any method other than FBI-standard calibrated gelatin, you are not replicating the standard protocol. That is acceptable — budget testing has legitimate value — but you must be clear in your documentation about what you did differently.

Describe your testing medium in detail. If using water jugs, specify the brand, size, whether they were new or reused, and how they were positioned (touching, spaced apart, on what surface). If using synthetic gel, specify the product, whether it was new or reused, and what temperature it was at the time of testing. These details allow anyone reading your data — including you, six months later — to understand the context and limitations of your results.

If you attempt to replicate FBI barrier tests using substitutes — shooting through denim draped over water jugs, for example — document exactly what materials you used, their dimensions, and how they were positioned. Understand that your results will not be directly comparable to FBI-protocol barrier testing, but the comparative data between different loads tested under identical substitute conditions remains useful.

The cardinal rule is consistency: if you test Load A through four layers of denim into water jugs at ten feet, and then test Load B for comparison, use the same four layers of denim, the same jug arrangement, and the same ten-foot distance. Variables you deliberately change (the ammunition being tested) produce data. Variables that change unintentionally (different barrier materials, different distances, different temperatures) produce noise.

**When to Trust Your Results — And When to Be Skeptical**

Ammunition testing by individuals or small groups is subject to limitations that professionally equipped laboratories can control but backyard experimenters cannot.

**Small sample sizes** are the most common problem. Firing one or two test rounds and drawing conclusions from them is unreliable. Professional testing uses five-shot strings as the minimum. If budget or material constraints limit you to fewer shots, acknowledge that your results are preliminary and that outlier performance from a single round may not represent the load's true average behavior.

**Uncontrolled variables** are the second major limitation. Temperature affects velocity. Barrel heating from repeated rapid fire affects pressure and velocity. Inconsistencies in the testing medium (one water jug filled more than another, gel at uneven temperature) affect penetration. If you notice a trend in your data — the third shot consistently penetrating deeper than the first two, for example — investigate whether you inadvertently changed something between shots.

**Confirmation bias** is the subtlest danger. If you are testing because you want to prove that your carry ammunition is "the best," you will unconsciously interpret ambiguous results in ways that confirm your preference. The antidote is to document everything objectively, perform blind tests when possible (have someone else label ammunition boxes so you do not know which load you are firing), and be willing to accept results that contradict your expectations.

If your informal testing contradicts published data from credible sources, do not immediately conclude that the published data is wrong. Examine your methodology first. Did you use the same barrel length? The same testing medium at the same temperature? Did you measure velocity to confirm that your ammunition matched published specifications? More often than not, discrepancies between backyard testing and professional results stem from uncontrolled variables in the informal test.

> ***Key Takeaway: Credible testing requires controlling variables, using adequate sample sizes, documenting everything, and acknowledging limitations. Your informal testing will almost never replicate the precision of professional laboratory work, but it can produce useful comparative data if you are systematic, honest about methodology, and skeptical of results that seem too good (or too bad) to be true. Test to learn, not to confirm what you already believe.***

**The Value of Imperfect Data**

Even informal testing conducted with budget equipment and substitute media has real value. It gives you hands-on experience with how bullets behave in soft tissue simulants. It allows you to compare loads side-by-side under identical conditions — which is often more useful than comparing published data from different sources tested under different protocols. It builds your understanding of what expansion looks like, what adequate penetration feels like when you measure it, and what variables genuinely matter.

Professional laboratory testing will always be more precise, more reproducible, and more credible when stakes are high. But waiting until you can afford professional testing means forfeiting years of learning opportunities. Start testing with what you can afford, document everything carefully, acknowledge your limitations honestly, and build your knowledge base one recovered bullet at a time. The skills you develop and the patterns you observe will make you a more informed consumer of published ammunition data — and a more confident evaluator of what belongs in your carry gun.
