**Chapter 11**

**Small-Caliber Defensive Options: When Recoil or Size Are Non-Negotiable Constraints**

*[IMAGE: A size-progression comparison photograph showing four handguns arranged left to right — a .22 LR rimfire pistol, a .22 Magnum revolver, a .25 ACP pocket pistol, and a 5.7x28mm semi-automatic — with loaded cartridges from each positioned beside their respective firearms, illustrating the spectrum of small-caliber defensive options]*

Every previous chapter in this book has focused on centerfire handgun cartridges in calibers traditionally considered adequate for defensive use — nine-millimeter and above. But the reality of concealed carry is that not every armed citizen can effectively manage the recoil of a nine-millimeter hollow-point, and not every defensive scenario permits carrying a firearm large enough to chamber it. Some people face legitimate physical limitations: arthritis, hand injuries, diminished grip strength, neurological conditions that affect motor control. Others prioritize deep concealment above all other factors and need the smallest possible firearm. For these individuals, the question is not whether small calibers are ideal — they demonstrably are not — but whether they are better than being unarmed.

This chapter addresses that question honestly. Small-caliber defensive ammunition — rimfire cartridges in .22 Long Rifle and .22 Magnum, along with the .25 ACP centerfire and the outlier 5.7x28mm — will never perform at the level of proper defensive centerfire cartridges. But they are not zero. Understanding their limitations, their strengths, and the tactical adjustments required to use them effectively is essential knowledge for anyone considering these options.

**The Fundamental Limitation: Permanent Wound Channel Size**

The physics are inescapable. A .22-caliber projectile creates a permanent wound channel approximately .22 inches in diameter if it does not expand, and perhaps .27 to .30 inches if it does. Compare that to a nine-millimeter hollow-point that routinely expands to .55 to .65 inches, or a .45-caliber projectile that starts at nearly half an inch before expansion. The permanent wound cavity volume — which, as we have established, is the primary factor in incapacitation through blood loss — is dramatically smaller with small-caliber ammunition.

Penetration with rimfire cartridges is generally adequate. Standard .22 Long Rifle ammunition penetrates fourteen to sixteen inches in bare gelatin with solid, non-expanding bullets. Hollow-point .22 LR loads that do expand may give up a couple inches of penetration in exchange for modest diameter increase. The .22 Magnum penetrates to similar depths with marginally better expansion potential at higher velocities. Penetration is not the problem. Wound channel diameter is.

The tactical implication is straightforward: if you are carrying a small-caliber defensive firearm, you cannot rely on one or two well-placed shots to incapacitate an attacker. You must accept that multiple hits — potentially many hits — will be necessary. This requirement places enormous demands on accuracy, speed, magazine capacity, and your ability to continue firing under extreme stress until the threat ceases.

**The .22 Long Rifle: The Baseline Rimfire Option**

The .22 Long Rifle is the most common cartridge in the world, available in more firearms than any other chambering, and produced in quantities measured in billions of rounds annually. It is inexpensive, produces minimal recoil, and in a quality firearm is capable of excellent accuracy.

As a defensive cartridge, it is marginal at best. Standard-velocity .22 LR loads produce approximately 100 to 120 foot-pounds of muzzle energy — one-third or less of what a nine-millimeter generates. High-velocity loads push this to perhaps 140 foot-pounds, still well below any centerfire defensive cartridge.

Hollow-point .22 LR ammunition exists, but expansion is unreliable. The velocities from typical handgun barrel lengths (four to six inches) are often insufficient to initiate consistent expansion, particularly if the bullet encounters any intermediate barrier such as heavy clothing. When expansion does occur, it is modest — a bullet that started at .223 inches might expand to .270 or .280 inches under ideal conditions.

The most common terminal behavior of a .22 LR bullet in tissue is to tumble. The projectile is relatively long compared to its diameter and is not gyroscopically stable enough in a dense medium to maintain point-forward flight. It yaws, turns sideways, and may come to rest base-first in the wound track. This tumbling creates a slightly larger disruption zone than a perfectly stable bullet would, but the effect is minimal.

If .22 Long Rifle is your only viable option — due to hand strength limitations, severe recoil sensitivity, or the need for absolute minimum size — the strategy must compensate for the cartridge's limitations. Use the most reliable, accurate ammunition you can find. Practice until you can deliver rapid, accurate fire to a target the size of a playing card at seven yards. Train to empty the magazine without hesitation, and have a reload immediately accessible. Accept that this is a high-volume-of-fire solution, not a one-shot answer.

**The .22 Winchester Magnum Rimfire: A Meaningful Step Up**

The .22 Magnum (also marketed as .22 WMR) uses a longer case than the .22 Long Rifle and is designed to fully enclose the bullet within the case mouth — unlike .22 LR, where the bullet protrudes beyond the case. This allows higher chamber pressures and correspondingly higher velocities.

From a rifle-length barrel, the .22 Magnum is genuinely impressive, producing velocities approaching or exceeding 2,000 feet per second. From a handgun barrel of four to six inches, the advantage over .22 Long Rifle is present but less dramatic — typical velocities in the 1,100 to 1,300 feet per second range, compared to 1,000 to 1,100 for high-velocity .22 LR loads.

The additional velocity makes a meaningful difference in expansion reliability. Several ammunition manufacturers produce defensive .22 Magnum loads with jacketed hollow-point bullets specifically engineered to expand at handgun velocities. These loads, when tested in bare gelatin, consistently deliver nine to eleven inches of penetration with expansion to approximately .30 inches — still small by centerfire standards, but a measurable improvement over .22 LR.

The trade-off is cost. .22 Magnum ammunition is substantially more expensive than .22 LR — practice full-metal-jacket loads cost roughly double what .22 LR does, and premium defensive hollow-points can cost three to four times as much. For someone on a limited budget who needs to practice extensively, this cost difference is not trivial.

The other trade-off is noise. The .22 Magnum is noticeably louder than .22 LR, which matters if you are concerned about hearing protection in a home-defense scenario or if noise sensitivity is part of what drove you to a small caliber in the first place.

For someone who cannot manage nine-millimeter recoil but can afford slightly higher ammunition costs, a quality .22 Magnum firearm loaded with purpose-designed defensive ammunition is a rational choice. Prioritize firearms with higher magazine capacity — a thirty-round magazine substantially improves your margin for error compared to a six-round revolver.

**The .25 ACP: Difficult to Recommend**

The .25 Automatic Colt Pistol (also known as 6.35mm Browning in European nomenclature) occupies an awkward middle ground. It offers centerfire reliability — the cartridge headspaces on the case mouth rather than relying on rimfire priming, which can improve feeding consistency in semi-automatic pistols. But its ballistic performance is nearly indistinguishable from .22 Long Rifle, and in some loadings, inferior.

Standard .25 ACP loads push a 50-grain full-metal-jacket bullet at approximately 750 to 800 feet per second, producing roughly 65 to 75 foot-pounds of muzzle energy. This is less energy than many .22 LR loads deliver. Penetration in gelatin is adequate — fourteen to sixteen inches with non-expanding bullets — but the permanent wound channel is .251 inches, only marginally larger than a .22-caliber projectile.

Expanding ammunition in .25 ACP exists but is rare and performs inconsistently. The velocities are simply too low to reliably initiate expansion in jacketed hollow-point bullets, and the small cavity dimensions leave little room for engineering solutions.

The firearms chambered in .25 ACP are often extremely small — pocket pistols designed for maximum concealability. This makes them difficult to shoot accurately and limits magazine capacity, often to six or seven rounds. If your defensive strategy with small calibers requires high-volume accurate fire, a platform that is hard to shoot well and holds few rounds is working against you.

For the cost of .25 ACP ammunition, you can purchase .22 Magnum defensive loads that deliver superior terminal performance in firearms that are no larger and substantially easier to shoot accurately. The .25 ACP served a role in an earlier era when reliable rimfire ammunition was less available and semi-automatic pistol feeding was less refined. Today, it offers no meaningful advantage over better alternatives.

**The 5.7x28mm: The Outlier**

The 5.7x28mm cartridge was developed for military applications — specifically, for use in compact personal defense weapons that occupy the role between a pistol and a submachine gun. It uses a small-caliber, lightweight, jacketed bullet pushed to high velocities from a bottlenecked case that is dimensionally similar to a pistol cartridge but operates at rifle-like pressures.

From the firearm for which it was designed — a compact weapon with a ten-inch barrel — the 5.7x28mm delivers impressive velocities, often exceeding 2,000 feet per second. From a pistol with a four-to-five-inch barrel, velocities drop to the 1,600 to 1,900 feet per second range, depending on the specific loading.

The cartridge's terminal performance is difficult to assess with confidence because the available data is limited and comes primarily from a small number of high-profile incidents rather than systematic testing. One widely analyzed case involved multiple casualties, some of whom sustained injuries that would not be expected from a "mere .22-caliber" projectile — broken femurs from extremity hits, rapid incapacitation from torso wounds. The specific ammunition used in that incident included both fragmenting projectiles designed for varmint hunting and more conventional designs, complicating the analysis.

The best interpretation of available evidence suggests that the 5.7x28mm, when loaded with appropriate ammunition, delivers terminal performance that is superior to .22 Magnum but still falls short of standard nine-millimeter defensive loads. The high velocity produces deeper penetration through barriers than rimfire cartridges achieve, and some loads demonstrate reliable expansion.

The practical obstacles are cost and platform size. Firearms chambered in 5.7x28mm are expensive — often two to three times the cost of comparable 9mm pistols. Magazines are expensive. Ammunition is expensive — roughly double the cost of nine-millimeter per round, and substantially more than .22 Magnum. The pistol grip, while not large in absolute terms, is bigger than most .22 rimfire options, which defeats the purpose if the reason you are considering a small caliber is hand size limitation.

For someone who has the budget, can manage the grip size, and genuinely cannot handle nine-millimeter recoil, a 5.7x28mm pistol loaded with quality full-metal-jacket ammunition (not fragmenting varmint bullets) represents the best-performing option in the small-caliber category. But it is a niche solution with a high buy-in cost.

> ***Key Takeaway: Small-caliber defensive ammunition — whether rimfire or the .25 ACP — will never deliver the terminal performance of proper defensive centerfire cartridges. If you carry a small caliber, accept that multiple rapid, accurate hits are mandatory. Train for high-volume fire, prioritize firearms with maximum magazine capacity, and use the best available defensive-designed ammunition. The .22 Magnum with modern expanding loads is the most cost-effective choice. The 5.7x28mm offers the best terminal performance but at substantial cost. The .25 ACP offers no meaningful advantage over better alternatives and is difficult to recommend.***

**Ammunition Selection for Small Calibers**

If you are committed to a small-caliber platform, ammunition selection follows different priorities than it does for centerfire defensive calibers.

For .22 Long Rifle, reliability and accuracy matter more than bullet design. Most .22 LR hollow-points will not expand consistently from handgun velocities, so choose the ammunition that feeds most reliably in your specific firearm and that you can shoot most accurately. High-velocity loads are marginally preferable to standard-velocity, but the difference is small. Practice ammunition should be identical to carry ammunition — the cost savings of bulk .22 LR are substantial, but consistency between practice and carry loads is more valuable.

For .22 Magnum, use purpose-designed defensive loads from manufacturers who publish gelatin test data. These loads use bullet designs specifically engineered to expand at the velocities a handgun barrel produces, not the much higher velocities achievable from rifles. Verify that your chosen load feeds reliably in your firearm and produces acceptable accuracy, then commit to it.

For 5.7x28mm, quality full-metal-jacket ammunition from the original cartridge developer is the safest choice. Expanding bullets exist, but the limited field data available suggests that over-expansion and fragmentation can compromise penetration. A non-expanding projectile that reliably reaches vital structures is preferable to a fragmenting bullet that stops shallow.

**The Tactical Reality: Volume of Fire Is Mandatory**

Regardless of which small-caliber option you choose, your defensive strategy must be built around the acceptance that multiple hits are required. One or two shots, even if perfectly placed, are unlikely to produce rapid incapacitation with a .22-caliber wound channel.

This means training to fire continuously until the threat stops. Not fire twice and assess. Not fire a controlled pair and wait for a reaction. Fire until the threat is no longer a threat. For many shooters accustomed to centerfire defensive training, this represents a significant mental adjustment.

Magazine capacity becomes critical. A six-shot revolver in .22 Magnum is minimally adequate. A ten-round .22 LR pistol is better. A thirty-round .22 Magnum pistol is meaningfully better still, not because you will need all thirty rounds, but because having them available reduces the consequences of missed shots and removes the immediate necessity of a reload under stress.

Immediate-access reloads are mandatory. If you carry a small-caliber defensive firearm, carrying at least one spare magazine or speedloader is not optional. Practice reloading under stress until it is automatic.

Accuracy standards must be tighter. With a nine-millimeter, "center mass" is an adequate aiming standard — a hit anywhere in the upper torso has reasonable probability of intersecting vital structures. With a .22-caliber wound channel, you need high thoracic hits. Train to deliver rapid, accurate fire to a target the size of a playing card, not a pie plate.

**When Small Calibers Are the Right Answer — And When They Are Not**

Small-caliber defensive firearms are a compromise, and like all compromises, they are right for some people in some circumstances and wrong for others.

They are a rational choice if you face genuine physical limitations that prevent you from controlling a nine-millimeter pistol, if you require absolute minimum size for deep concealment in contexts where larger firearms are impractical, or if you are working with someone (an elderly parent, a spouse with arthritis) who cannot safely handle anything larger.

They are the wrong choice if your only reason for selecting one is perceived recoil aversion that could be overcome with proper training, grip technique, and graduated exposure. Many people who believe they "cannot handle" nine-millimeter recoil have never fired one from a modern, full-sized pistol with good sights and proper instruction. Start there before concluding that a small caliber is your only option.

They are also the wrong choice if your self-assessment of defensive needs suggests a high probability of encountering dedicated, determined threats or multiple attackers. Small calibers are minimal tools for minimal threats. If your risk profile is elevated, invest the training time to build recoil tolerance for a proper defensive caliber.

The bottom line: a .22 Magnum in your pocket is better than a nine-millimeter locked in your safe at home because it was too big to carry comfortably. But a nine-millimeter that you trained yourself to shoot accurately and carry consistently is better than a .22 Magnum. Choose the option that matches your real constraints, not your assumed constraints, and then train to use it to its maximum potential within its real limitations.

> ***Key Takeaway: Small-caliber defensive firearms are tools of last resort, not first choice. If genuine physical limitations or concealment requirements make centerfire calibers impractical, choose the most effective small-caliber option you can manage (preferably .22 Magnum or 5.7x28mm with quality defensive ammunition), prioritize maximum magazine capacity, and train relentlessly for rapid, accurate, high-volume fire. Accept that this is a high-risk, high-skill solution that demands more from you, not less, than carrying a proper defensive caliber would.***
