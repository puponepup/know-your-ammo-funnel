**Chapter 12**

**The .32 and .380: Pocket Pistol Calibers That Still Have a Place**

*[IMAGE: A classic pocket pistol in .380 ACP positioned beside a modern subcompact 9mm of similar dimensions — both shown with their respective defensive ammunition loaded and spare magazines beside them — illustrating the size-versus-performance trade-off that defines the pocket-pistol debate]*

There was an era — spanning from the early 1900s through the 1970s — when slipping a pocket pistol chambered in .32 or .380 into a coat pocket was considered a perfectly adequate defensive measure. Professionals carried them as backup firearms. Civilians relied on them for daily protection. They were not seen as marginal compromises but as legitimate defensive tools appropriate to the threats of the time.

The modern concealed-carry culture has largely moved past that perspective. Today, carrying anything smaller than nine-millimeter risks being dismissed as naive or under-armed, and the ballistic case for that dismissal is strong. A .380 produces roughly half the energy of a nine-millimeter. A .32 Auto produces even less. Neither reaches the FBI twelve-inch minimum penetration standard with expanding ammunition. Both struggle to achieve reliable expansion at all.

And yet, these cartridges continue to sell in substantial volumes, firearms chambered for them continue to be manufactured, and ammunition companies continue investing in engineering better-performing loads. This is not market irrationality. It is the recognition that for some people, in some contexts, a pocket-sized pistol in .32 or .380 is the most firearm they can realistically carry — and a marginal firearm carried consistently beats an ideal firearm left at home because it was too big or too heavy.

**The .32 ACP: Ballistically Equivalent to Rimfire**

The .32 Automatic Colt Pistol (also known as 7.65mm Browning in European designation) was one of the earliest successful semi-automatic pistol cartridges. Developed in the early twentieth century for compact pocket pistols, it offered centerfire reliability in a package only marginally larger than rimfire designs.

Standard .32 ACP ammunition pushes a 71-grain full-metal-jacket bullet at approximately 900 feet per second, producing roughly 130 foot-pounds of muzzle energy. This is comparable to — and in some loadings, less than — what a high-velocity .22 Long Rifle delivers. Penetration in bare gelatin is adequate, typically fourteen to sixteen inches with non-expanding bullets, but the permanent wound channel is only .312 inches wide.

Hollow-point ammunition in .32 ACP exists but performs inconsistently. The velocities from typical pocket-pistol barrel lengths (two to three and a half inches) are marginal for initiating reliable expansion in jacketed hollow-points. Some loads expand; others do not. When expansion does occur, it is modest — a bullet might grow from .312 inches to .35 or .40 inches under ideal conditions in bare gelatin. Pass that same bullet through heavy clothing and expansion becomes even less predictable.

The most honest assessment of the .32 ACP as a defensive cartridge is this: it delivers terminal performance roughly equivalent to .22 Magnum but at higher cost and with less ammunition availability. If you already own a .32 pistol and it is your only option, load it with the most accurate ammunition you can find — whether that is hollow-point or full-metal-jacket is secondary to whether you can hit what you aim at — and train extensively. But if you are making a purchasing decision from scratch, the .22 Magnum or the .380 are better choices at similar or lower total cost.

**The .380 ACP: Where Expansion and Penetration Compete**

The .380 Automatic (also known as 9mm Kurz, 9mm Short, or 9x17mm) represents a meaningful step up from the .32 in both size and performance. Standard loads push a 90 to 95-grain bullet at approximately 900 to 950 feet per second, producing 170 to 190 foot-pounds of muzzle energy — still well below the 350+ foot-pounds that nine-millimeter defensive loads routinely achieve, but a legitimate improvement over smaller pocket-pistol calibers.

The critical challenge with .380 ammunition is that it sits precisely at the boundary where expansion and adequate penetration become competing priorities rather than compatible goals. The cartridge has *just enough* velocity and energy to expand a properly designed hollow-point bullet, or *just enough* momentum to penetrate to adequate depth, but achieving both simultaneously is extremely difficult.

Modern defensive .380 hollow-point loads demonstrate this tension clearly. A well-engineered 90-grain expanding bullet may achieve impressive expansion — opening to .50 or even .55 inches in bare gelatin — but that expansion typically arrests penetration at nine to eleven inches. The bullet expends so much energy deforming that it stops short of the twelve-inch minimum the FBI considers necessary for reliable vital-structure access.

Conversely, a 95-grain full-metal-jacket .380 load penetrates to sixteen or even twenty inches — well past the minimum — but creates only a .355-inch wound channel. No expansion, minimal tissue disruption, high probability of complete pass-through with energy wasted on overpenetration.

**The Institutional Approach: Prioritizing Penetration**

The trade-off between expansion and penetration has led at least one major metropolitan law enforcement agency to adopt a counterintuitive policy for backup and off-duty .380 firearms: mandatory use of full-metal-jacket ammunition, with expanding hollow-points prohibited.

The reasoning is sound within their operational framework. An officer engaging a threat may need to fire through heavy winter clothing, leather jackets, or other intermediate barriers that will further reduce the already-marginal penetration of an expanding .380 bullet. By mandating non-expanding full-metal-jacket loads, the agency ensures that penetration remains adequate even in worst-case scenarios — accepting the trade-off of a narrow wound channel in exchange for guaranteed depth.

Whether this logic applies to your situation depends on your individual context. If you live in a cold climate where heavy winter clothing is worn for months at a time, if barrier penetration is a realistic concern in your defensive scenario, or if you prioritize worst-case reliability over best-case performance, the full-metal-jacket approach has merit.

Conversely, if your most likely scenario involves close range against a lightly clothed attacker in a warm climate, and if you are confident in your ability to deliver multiple accurate hits quickly, an expanding hollow-point that creates a wider wound channel at the cost of marginal penetration may serve you better.

There is no universal right answer. The .380 forces you to choose your priority.

**Ammunition Selection Strategies for .32 and .380**

If you carry a .32 ACP firearm, your ammunition selection is straightforward: use whatever feeds most reliably in your specific pistol and produces the best accuracy. The terminal performance differences between .32 ACP loads are too small to be meaningful. Reliability and accuracy — the factors you control through ammunition selection and training — are what matter. If hollow-point ammunition feeds reliably and shoots accurately, use it. If your pistol functions better with full-metal-jacket loads, use those instead.

For the .380, the decision is more nuanced. Modern defensive .380 loads fall into two distinct categories:

**Expansion-optimized loads** use lighter bullets (80 to 90 grains) at maximum velocities, engineered to expand reliably in bare gelatin and through light barriers. These loads typically penetrate nine to eleven inches while expanding to .50 to .60 inches. They maximize wound channel diameter at the cost of marginal penetration depth.

**Penetration-optimized loads** use full-weight bullets (95 to 100 grains) in either non-expanding full-metal-jacket designs or controlled-expansion hollow-points engineered to limit expansion and prioritize depth. These loads penetrate fourteen to eighteen inches but create narrower wound channels.

Test multiple options in your specific firearm. Pay attention not just to advertised ballistic data but to what actually functions in your gun and what you can shoot accurately. A load that produces spectacular gelatin results but does not feed reliably in your pistol is useless. A load that penetrates perfectly but shoots four inches low from your sights requires either sight adjustment or a different ammunition choice.

> ***Key Takeaway: The .380 ACP forces a choice between expansion (creating a wider wound channel but risking inadequate penetration) and depth (ensuring vital-structure access but creating a narrower wound channel). Modern defensive hollow-points optimize for expansion but rarely achieve FBI minimum penetration. Full-metal-jacket loads guarantee depth but eliminate expansion. Choose based on your most likely scenario — and whichever you choose, train extensively, because marginal terminal performance demands excellence in shot placement and volume of fire.***

**Platform Considerations: Bigger Is Better**

If you have committed to carrying a .32 or .380 pistol, do not compound the caliber compromise by choosing the absolute smallest firearm available. Ultra-compact pocket pistols in these calibers — two-inch barrels, abbreviated grips, minimal sight radius — are difficult to shoot accurately and punishing to shoot extensively in practice.

The velocity loss from a two-inch barrel compared to a three-and-a-half-inch barrel is measurable and meaningful for ammunition that is already operating at the margins of expansion and penetration performance. The diminished grip area makes recoil control harder, not easier, despite the cartridge's light recoil. The minimal sights make precise shot placement — which is mandatory with small wound channels — substantially more difficult.

Choose the largest .380 pistol you can comfortably conceal and carry. A pistol with a three-to-four-inch barrel, a full grip, and decent sights will produce better velocities, better accuracy, and better hit probability than a micro-compact alternative. The difference in concealability is measured in fractions of an inch. The difference in shootability is substantial.

Higher capacity is valuable. A six-round .380 is minimally adequate. An eight-to-ten-round .380 gives you more margin for misses and more rounds available before a reload is required. Given that small-caliber defensive use requires high volume of fire, more rounds in the gun is a direct advantage.

**The Modern Reality: .380 Versus Compact 9mm**

The relentless drive toward smaller nine-millimeter firearms has narrowed the size gap between .380 pocket pistols and compact 9mm platforms to the point where they are, in some models, nearly indistinguishable. This has led many trainers and commentators to argue that the .380 is obsolete — that if you can carry a .380, you can carry a 9mm of the same size, and the 9mm delivers dramatically superior terminal performance.

This argument is partially correct but incomplete. A nine-millimeter pistol built to .380 dimensions is substantially more punishing in felt recoil. The lighter slide and smaller frame do less to absorb and distribute the recoil impulse, concentrating it in the shooter's hand. For someone with hand strength limitations, arthritis, or recoil sensitivity, the difference between shooting a .380 comfortably and shooting a micro-9mm with gritted teeth is real and meaningful.

The risk of developing a flinch — an involuntary anticipatory response to recoil that degrades accuracy — is higher with uncomfortable guns. A .380 that you can shoot confidently and accurately is more useful than a 9mm that you shoot reluctantly and poorly.

That said: if you can manage the recoil of a compact nine-millimeter without discomfort or accuracy degradation, there is no ballistic reason to choose a .380 instead. The nine-millimeter delivers objectively better terminal performance in every measurable category. The .380 is a compromise born of necessity, not preference.

> ***Key Takeaway: The .32 ACP offers no meaningful advantage over better alternatives and is difficult to recommend for new purchases. The .380 ACP remains viable for those who face genuine size or recoil constraints that make compact 9mm pistols impractical. If you carry a .380, choose the largest platform you can conceal, test multiple ammunition options to find the best balance of expansion and penetration for your scenario, and train relentlessly for rapid, accurate, high-volume fire. A .380 is a tool for people who cannot carry anything larger — not a first choice for those who can.***
