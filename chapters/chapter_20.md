**Chapter 20**

**The .45s: Big Bore Effectiveness, Big Bore Trade-Offs**

*[IMAGE: Two classic .45-caliber firearms side by side — a 1911-pattern semi-automatic pistol in .45 ACP and a large-frame single-action revolver in .45 Colt — both shown with modern defensive ammunition, illustrating the parallel evolution of these two iconic American cartridges that share bullet diameter but serve different roles]*

The .45-caliber defensive cartridges — the .45 ACP (Automatic Colt Pistol) and the .45 Colt revolver round — represent the traditional American approach to defensive handgun ammunition: large bullets moving at moderate velocities, delivering terminal performance through mass and frontal area rather than extreme velocity. Both cartridges predate the FBI testing protocols by decades, both were designed in eras when hollow-point bullet technology did not exist, and both remain viable defensive options in 2026 despite being over a century old.

The reason for their persistence is straightforward: a .452-inch-diameter bullet creates a permanent wound channel that is meaningfully larger than what nine-millimeter or .40-caliber projectiles produce, even before expansion is considered. When modern hollow-point technology is applied to these large-diameter platforms, the result is impressive terminal performance that easily exceeds FBI standards with even modest bullet designs.

The cost is platform size. Both .45 cartridges require large firearms — the .45 ACP needs pistol frames designed around the cartridge's length and width, and the .45 Colt requires full-sized revolver frames. The capacity disadvantage compared to nine-millimeter is significant, and the shootability advantage that proponents claim is overstated. This chapter examines both cartridges honestly, acknowledging their strengths while addressing the trade-offs that make them niche choices in contemporary defensive carry.

**The .45 ACP: Efficient Design, Enduring Performance**

The .45 ACP was developed in 1905 for semi-automatic pistol use, refined through military trials, and adopted by the U.S. military in 1911 as the cartridge for what became one of the most iconic firearms in American history. The design was deliberate and thoughtful: a rimless case optimized for reliable feeding, a bullet seated deeply into the case to minimize overall cartridge length, and chamber pressures kept moderate (21,000 PSI maximum) to ensure function in the recoil-operated pistols of the era.

The original loading — a 230-grain full-metal-jacket ball round at approximately 850 feet per second — produced roughly 370 foot-pounds of muzzle energy. This is comparable to modern nine-millimeter defensive loads, but delivered through a substantially larger-diameter projectile. The permanent wound channel from a non-expanding .45 ACP ball round is .452 inches — fifty percent wider than a non-expanding nine-millimeter bullet. That diameter advantage matters, and it is why the .45 ACP earned its reputation for effectiveness even with crude ammunition technology.

Modern .45 ACP hollow-points transform that baseline performance into something genuinely impressive. Quality defensive loads routinely expand to .65 to .80 inches, penetrate thirteen to sixteen inches, and pass FBI barrier tests with minimal degradation. The large bullet diameter provides substantial volume for hollow-point cavity design, and the moderate velocities (850 to 950 feet per second for most defensive loads) fall within the sweet spot where jacketed hollow-points expand reliably without fragmenting.

The cartridge is also remarkably efficient. The bullet occupies most of the case volume, leaving just enough room for the powder charge needed to achieve target velocities. This efficiency means the .45 ACP produces its performance without requiring excessive chamber pressure, which translates to manageable recoil in properly designed firearms and long service life for pistols.

**The Platform Problem: Size, Weight, and Capacity**

The .45 ACP's terminal performance has never been in question. The challenge is platform. A .45 ACP pistol must be built on a frame large enough to accommodate the cartridge's dimensions and manage its recoil. Even the most compact .45 ACP pistols are noticeably larger than subcompact nine-millimeter guns, and the magazine capacity is substantially lower — typically six to eight rounds in a compact .45 compared to ten to fifteen rounds in a comparably sized 9mm.

For full-sized duty pistols, the capacity gap is even more pronounced. A duty-sized 9mm holds fifteen to nineteen rounds. A duty-sized .45 ACP holds eight to ten rounds in single-stack magazines or twelve to fourteen in double-stack configurations. The double-stack .45 pistols achieve higher capacity but at the cost of grip circumference that many shooters — particularly those with smaller hands — find difficult to manage.

The recoil is often described as "slow and rolling" compared to the "snappy" recoil of nine-millimeter or .40 S&W, and in full-sized, all-steel pistols, this characterization is accurate. The .45 ACP pushes backward rather than flipping upward, and experienced shooters often find it easier to manage than the sharper impulse of smaller calibers. But this advantage largely disappears in compact, lightweight pistols where the reduced mass concentrates the recoil impulse.

If you choose the .45 ACP, accept that you are choosing a large platform with meaningful capacity limitations in exchange for a permanent wound channel that is measurably wider than what smaller calibers produce. Whether that trade-off is worthwhile depends on your priorities and your ability to conceal and carry a larger firearm comfortably.

> ***Key Takeaway: The .45 ACP delivers excellent terminal performance through large bullet diameter and well-engineered hollow-point designs. Any quality modern .45 ACP defensive load will easily pass FBI protocols and deliver expansion in the .65-.80-inch range with adequate penetration. The limitation is not ammunition performance but platform size and capacity. Choose the .45 ACP if you value its specific advantages enough to accept a larger gun with fewer rounds. For most carriers, a modern nine-millimeter in a more compact platform with higher capacity is the better choice.***

**Ammunition Selection for .45 ACP: Weight Matters Less Than You Think**

A persistent debate among .45 ACP carriers centers on bullet weight: should you carry traditional 230-grain loads, or lighter 185 to 200-grain options?

The answer is that modern bullet engineering has made the weight distinction largely irrelevant for defensive purposes. Quality 185-grain hollow-points deliver terminal performance that is functionally equivalent to 230-grain loads in FBI testing. They expand to similar diameters, penetrate to similar depths, and retain similar percentages of their original weight. The velocity difference (185-grain loads typically travel 50 to 100 feet per second faster) does not translate into meaningful terminal performance advantages.

Choose based on what your specific pistol shoots most accurately and feeds most reliably, not on theoretical performance differences that gelatin testing cannot consistently distinguish. If your pistol prefers 230-grain ammunition, use it. If it shoots 185-grain loads more accurately, use those instead.

The +P question — whether to use ammunition loaded to elevated pressures for increased velocity — is similarly straightforward. Standard-pressure .45 ACP ammunition already delivers terminal performance that exceeds FBI minimums. The +P loads offer marginally better barrier penetration and slightly larger expansion, but the difference is modest and comes at the cost of increased recoil and accelerated wear. Unless you have a specific need for the additional performance (you anticipate barrier encounters, you are carrying a full-sized pistol that manages the recoil easily), standard-pressure ammunition is adequate.

**The .45 Colt: Revolver Power From a Different Era**

The .45 Colt was introduced in 1873 for use in the Colt Single Action Army revolver — the iconic "Peacemaker" of Western legend. It predates the .45 ACP by more than thirty years and was designed for black powder propellants, which means its case is substantially longer and its operating pressure is substantially lower (14,000 PSI compared to the ACP's 21,000 PSI).

Despite the lower pressure, the .45 Colt delivers impressive terminal performance with modern ammunition. A 225 to 255-grain jacketed hollow-point at 750 to 850 feet per second produces energy comparable to .45 ACP while creating even larger expanded diameters due to the heavier bullet mass. Penetration is deep and consistent, expansion is reliable, and the moderate velocities ensure that bullets hold together even when encountering barriers.

The .45 Colt's low operating pressure has a practical advantage: revolvers chambered for it experience minimal wear and can deliver a lifetime of service with minimal maintenance. The recoil is moderate and manageable from large-frame revolvers, making practice pleasant and extended shooting sessions comfortable.

The limitation is the same one that affects all revolver cartridges in contemporary defensive use: you are carrying a large, heavy, six-shot firearm with slow reloads. A .45 Colt revolver weighs forty-plus ounces, holds six rounds, and requires speedloaders or loose cartridges for reloading. A modern semi-automatic in nine-millimeter weighs less, holds more than double the ammunition, and reloads with a magazine change that takes a fraction of the time.

If you carry a .45 Colt revolver, you are making a deliberate choice to prioritize the revolver platform's specific advantages (reliability, simplicity, durability) over capacity and reload speed. That choice can be rational — particularly for those who value classic firearms, who have extensive revolver training, or who operate in environments where revolver reliability advantages matter. But it is a choice that comes with real tactical costs.

> ***Key Takeaway: Both .45 cartridges deliver terminal performance that easily exceeds defensive requirements with even basic hollow-point ammunition. The .45 ACP offers semi-automatic platform advantages but requires large pistols with limited capacity. The .45 Colt delivers impressive ballistics from revolvers with low operating pressures and long service life, but capacity and reload speed are severely limited. Choose based on platform preferences and mission requirements, not on chasing marginal ammunition performance differences — any quality .45 defensive load will work well.***
