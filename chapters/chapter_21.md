**Chapter 21**

**Handgun Hunting Ammunition: When Defense Meets Game**

*[IMAGE: A hunting-configured revolver in .44 Magnum with a scope mounted, displayed alongside heavyweight hard-cast hunting ammunition and a whitetail deer silhouette target showing vital zone placement — illustrating the precision and power requirements for ethical handgun hunting]*

This book has focused primarily on defensive ammunition selection — cartridges and loads optimized for stopping human threats at close range. But many concealed carriers also hunt, and the question of whether a defensive handgun and defensive ammunition can serve double duty as a hunting tool is worth addressing.

The short answer is: for deer-sized game, yes, with proper ammunition selection and realistic assessment of your accuracy limitations. For larger or more dangerous game, specialized hunting ammunition and platforms are required, and that topic exceeds this book's scope.

This chapter provides guidance on using defensive-caliber handguns for hunting deer-sized animals, clarifies what ammunition characteristics matter for hunting versus defense, and establishes when you should recognize that your defensive sidearm is inadequate for the game you are considering.

**The Size Threshold: Deer as the Baseline**

A mature whitetail deer weighs approximately 150 to 250 pounds — roughly comparable to an adult human in mass, though with different skeletal structure and vital zone placement. This size equivalence means that cartridges adequate for defensive use against humans are, in principle, adequate for hunting deer-sized game.

The key qualifier is "in principle." Defensive shooting and hunting impose different requirements. In a defensive encounter, shots are typically taken at close range (seven to fifteen feet), the target is usually facing you with vital zones relatively accessible, and follow-up shots are possible if the first shot does not immediately incapacitate. In hunting, shots may be taken at substantially greater distances (twenty-five to seventy-five yards for handgun hunting), the animal may be angled or quartering in ways that require the bullet to penetrate through non-vital tissue before reaching the heart or lungs, and follow-up shots on a wounded animal that has run into dense cover may be impossible.

These differences mean that hunting ammunition must prioritize deep, straight-line penetration over rapid expansion. A defensive hollow-point optimized to expand quickly and dump energy in twelve to fourteen inches is poorly suited to punching through the shoulder blade of a quartering deer to reach the vitals. You need bullets that will penetrate deeply, hold together when they strike bone, and create wound channels that lead to rapid blood loss and quick, humane kills.

**The Minimum Threshold: Start at .357 Magnum**

For deer-sized game, the practical minimum is the .357 Magnum loaded with heavy bonded-core or hard-cast bullets. Lighter calibers — nine-millimeter, .38 Special, .380 ACP — lack the bullet mass and energy to reliably deliver the deep penetration that hunting requires. You might kill a deer with a nine-millimeter hollow-point under ideal conditions, but "might" and "ideal conditions" are not acceptable standards for ethical hunting.

A .357 Magnum pushing a 158-grain bonded jacketed soft-point or a 180-grain hard-cast bullet at 1,200+ feet per second delivers energy in the 500 to 600 foot-pound range and penetration that can reach twenty inches or more. This is adequate for broadside or slightly quartering shots on deer, provided you have the accuracy to place your shot in the vital zone.

From there, you scale upward based on what you can shoot accurately. The .44 Magnum with 240-grain hunting bullets delivers substantially more energy and penetration than the .357. The .45 Colt with heavyweight hard-cast loads offers similar performance. The 10mm Auto with 200-grain hard-cast bullets sits between the .357 and .44 in capability. All of these are adequate for deer-sized game. All of them are also substantially harder to shoot accurately at extended range than smaller calibers.

> ***Key Takeaway: For deer-sized game, start at .357 Magnum with bonded-core or hard-cast bullets and scale upward based on what you can shoot accurately at hunting distances. Defensive hollow-points optimized for FBI-protocol testing are poorly suited to hunting — they expand too rapidly and penetrate inadequately through heavy bone and muscle. Use hunting-specific ammunition designed for deep penetration and structural integrity, and verify your ability to place shots accurately at realistic hunting ranges before taking to the field.***

**The Accuracy Problem: Recoil Versus Precision**

The single biggest obstacle to successful handgun hunting is not ammunition performance or terminal ballistics — it is your ability to deliver a well-placed shot to the vital zone of an animal at twenty-five, fifty, or seventy-five yards. This is exponentially harder than defensive shooting at seven yards, and it is made harder still by recoil.

A .44 Magnum produces impressive terminal ballistics, but if the recoil causes you to flinch or if you cannot hold the sights steady enough to guarantee a vital-zone hit at fifty yards, you are better served by a .357 Magnum that you can shoot accurately. An adequate cartridge with precise shot placement kills cleanly. A powerful cartridge with marginal shot placement produces wounded animals that escape to die slowly.

Before committing to handgun hunting, spend extensive time at the range verifying your maximum effective range. Set up deer-sized vital-zone targets at twenty-five, fifty, and seventy-five yards. Fire from realistic field positions — standing unsupported, kneeling, using improvised rests. Determine the maximum distance at which you can consistently place five out of five shots into the vital zone. That distance — not your optimistic guess, but your demonstrated capability — is your maximum ethical hunting range.

If that range is twenty-five yards, you can hunt successfully in environments that permit close stalking. If it is fifty yards, you have more flexibility. If it is less than twenty-five yards, you should reconsider whether handgun hunting is appropriate for you, or whether you need to invest more training time before pursuing game.

**For Larger Game: Specialized Knowledge Required**

Deer-sized game represent the upper limit of what this book addresses. For larger animals — elk, moose, bear, dangerous African game — specialized ammunition selection and platform choices are required that exceed defensive handgun considerations entirely.

If you intend to hunt large or dangerous game with a handgun, consult resources specifically dedicated to that topic. The bullet designs, velocities, and platform requirements for taking a thousand-pound animal or defending against a charging predator are different in kind, not just degree, from what defensive ammunition provides.

The general principle remains: use the heaviest, hardest-cast bullet with the widest flat nose that your firearm can stabilize, loaded to the maximum velocity you can manage accurately. For truly large game, this typically means cartridges like the .454 Casull, .460 S&W Magnum, .500 S&W Magnum, or similar big-bore hunting rounds — not the defensive calibers covered in this book.

> ***Key Takeaway: Handgun hunting imposes requirements that differ fundamentally from defensive use. Deep penetration, structural integrity through bone, and extreme precision at extended range are mandatory. If you hunt deer-sized game with a defensive-caliber handgun, use hunting-specific ammunition and verify your accuracy at realistic distances. For larger or dangerous game, recognize when your defensive sidearm is inadequate and invest in purpose-built hunting firearms and ammunition designed for the task. Ethical hunting demands that you know your limitations and operate within them — not push the boundaries and hope for the best.***
