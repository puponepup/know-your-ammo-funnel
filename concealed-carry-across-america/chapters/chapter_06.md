**Chapter 6**

**Open Carry, Interstate Transport, and Handling Traffic Stops**

*[IMAGE: A responsible-looking man in casual clothing walking through a small-town Main Street area with a holstered handgun clearly visible on his hip. Daytime, normal community setting. Illustrates open carry in a gun-friendly jurisdiction.]*

Carrying a firearm openly on your person — what the law calls "open carry" — is legal in many states. So is transporting firearms through jurisdictions where your weapons would otherwise be prohibited. But both practices come with risks that go far beyond the written statute. This chapter covers the legal frameworks governing visible carry, the federal protections that allow you to move firearms across hostile territory, and the critical skill every armed traveler needs: how to handle a traffic stop without turning a routine encounter into an arrest.

**Open Carry: Legal Does Not Always Mean Advisable**

In states where open carry is lawful, any eligible person may carry a handgun in a clearly visible holster while going about their daily business. No concealment is required, and in many of these jurisdictions, no permit is needed either. For travelers who lack a recognized carry permit in a given state, open carry may be the only legal method of being armed in public.

However, legality and practicality are two different things. Openly displaying a firearm in an urban environment — even in a state where it is perfectly legal — will almost certainly attract attention. Some of that attention will come from concerned citizens who may call law enforcement. Some of it will come from law enforcement officers who, regardless of the law, may view an openly armed individual as a potential threat.

The result is a paradox: exercising a legal right can lead to encounters that feel like enforcement actions. Officers may approach, question, and even temporarily detain an open carrier under the justification of investigating a citizen complaint. While no crime has been committed, the disruption to your travel and the stress of the encounter are real.

For these reasons, armed travelers who choose open carry should take deliberate steps to minimize friction. Use a quality retention holster that is clearly visible and secure. Avoid reaching for or adjusting the firearm in public. Remain calm and cooperative if approached by law enforcement, and be prepared to calmly explain the legal basis for your carry.

Open carry is subject to the same location-based restrictions that govern concealed carry. Schools, government buildings, courthouses, and private properties that have posted prohibitions are off-limits regardless of how the firearm is carried.

**The McClure-Volkmer Act: Your Federal Shield for Interstate Transport**

One of the most important legal protections available to armed travelers is a federal statute passed in 1986 as part of the Firearms Owners' Protection Act, commonly known as the McClure-Volkmer Act. This law provides a critical safe harbor for gun owners who need to transport firearms through states where those weapons would otherwise be illegal.

The protection works like this: if your firearm is legal at your point of origin and legal at your final destination, you may drive through any state in between — even one that would otherwise arrest you for possessing that weapon — provided you follow specific transport requirements.

Those requirements are strict and non-negotiable:

- The firearm must be completely unloaded — no rounds in the chamber, no rounds in the magazine if it is inserted into the weapon.
- The firearm must be stored in a locked, hard-sided container.
- The container must be placed in the trunk of the vehicle. If the vehicle has no trunk — such as an SUV, truck, or minivan — the container must be in a locked storage compartment that is not readily accessible from the passenger area. The glove box and center console do not qualify.
- Ammunition must be stored separately from the firearm.

If all four conditions are met and you are genuinely in transit — meaning you are driving through the state, not stopping for extended periods — you are protected under federal law.

**The Limits of Federal Protection**

The McClure-Volkmer shield is powerful, but it has boundaries that travelers must understand.

First, the protection only applies to transit. If you stop for anything beyond the bare minimum — fuel, food, or an emergency — you may be considered to have "arrived" in the restrictive state, at which point its laws apply to you in full. Stopping to visit a friend, attend an event, or spend the night at a hotel can void your federal protection entirely. Some experienced travelers carry pre-printed hotel or campground reservations for their actual destination as evidence that they are in transit and not stopping permanently.

Second, the law explicitly covers firearms and ammunition. It does not protect accessories such as magazines that exceed a state's capacity limit, certain types of ammunition that may be restricted, or other regulated components. Courts have not fully resolved whether items like suppressors or standard-capacity magazines fall under the statute's umbrella. The safest assumption is that they do not.

Third, enforcement is inconsistent. Some states — particularly those with aggressive firearms enforcement — have been known to arrest interstate travelers even when the conditions of McClure-Volkmer appear to be met, forcing the individual to assert the federal defense after the fact. Being legally right and being arrested are not mutually exclusive, and the burden of proving compliance often falls on the traveler rather than the state.

**Traffic Stops: The Moment of Truth**

Every armed traveler will eventually be pulled over. Whether it is a speeding infraction, a broken taillight, or a random checkpoint, the interaction between an armed citizen and a law enforcement officer during a traffic stop is one of the most high-stakes encounters in the concealed carry world.

The overarching principles are simple: stay calm, keep your hands visible, follow instructions, and do not volunteer information that could be used against you.

When you see the lights behind you, pull over safely and promptly. Turn off the engine. Place both hands on the steering wheel where the approaching officer can see them. Do not reach for your license, registration, or anything else until the officer arrives and asks you to do so.

**Should You Disclose That You Are Armed?**

This is the single most debated question among concealed carriers, and the answer depends entirely on which state you are in.

Some states impose a "duty to immediately inform" — meaning you are legally required to tell the officer that you are carrying a firearm the moment the interaction begins, before any questions are asked. Failure to comply with this requirement is a separate criminal offense that can result in arrest, even if your carry is otherwise perfectly legal. These states are clearly noted in the individual profiles throughout this guide.

Other states require disclosure only if the officer directly asks whether you are armed. In these jurisdictions, you have no obligation to volunteer the information, but you must answer truthfully if the question is posed.

A third group of states imposes no duty to inform whatsoever. In these jurisdictions, your carry status is your private business unless it becomes relevant to an investigation.

Regardless of the legal requirement, many experienced carriers recommend disclosing proactively during any traffic stop, particularly if you are carrying on your person. Doing so establishes that you are a licensed, law-abiding individual who is cooperating fully. It reduces the risk of a surprise discovery — which almost always escalates the encounter — and it sets a tone of transparency that most officers will appreciate.

If you choose to disclose, do so calmly and clearly: "Officer, I want you to know that I have a valid carry permit and I am currently armed. How would you like me to proceed?" Then wait for instructions. Do not reach for the weapon, point at it, or make any movement toward it until directed.

**Consent to Search: Protecting Your Rights**

During a traffic stop, an officer may ask for permission to search your vehicle. This is a request, not an order. Unless the officer has probable cause to believe the vehicle contains evidence of a crime, you have the right to refuse.

Many people consent to searches out of nervousness or a desire to appear cooperative. This is a mistake. Granting consent waives critical legal protections and gives the officer broad authority to inspect every compartment, bag, and container in the vehicle. If a firearm is discovered during a consensual search in a state where you may not have been in full compliance, you have effectively handed the evidence to the prosecution.

The appropriate response is polite but firm: "Officer, I do not consent to a search of my vehicle." If the officer has legitimate grounds to search anyway, they will proceed regardless of your response. But if they do not, a refusal preserves your legal position completely.

Officers are also permitted to conduct a limited "frisk" of your person and the area immediately around the driver's seat if they have reasonable suspicion that you may be armed and dangerous. This type of protective search does not require your consent, but it cannot extend to the full vehicle without probable cause.

**License Plate Surveillance: A Hidden Risk**

Armed travelers should be aware that some states have adopted automated license plate reader technology that can flag vehicles registered to concealed carry permit holders. Reports have confirmed that at least one jurisdiction has used this capability to initiate traffic stops against out-of-state drivers whose records indicated they held concealed carry permits — even when no traffic violation had occurred.

While the legality of this practice is debatable, the practical reality is that your permit status may be known to law enforcement before the officer even approaches your window. This is another reason why a calm, transparent, and cooperative demeanor during traffic stops is essential.

> ***Key Takeaway: Open carry is legal in many states but attracts scrutiny. Federal law protects interstate transport of firearms through restrictive states, but only if the weapon is unloaded, locked, and stored in the trunk with ammunition separate. During traffic stops, know your state's disclosure requirements, keep your hands visible, and never consent to a vehicle search. The way you handle the first 30 seconds of a law enforcement encounter can determine whether you drive away or get arrested.***
