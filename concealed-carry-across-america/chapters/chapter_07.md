**Chapter 7**

**Off-Limits: Restricted Areas and Special Locations**

*[IMAGE: A montage of "no firearms" signs in front of various locations — a federal courthouse, a post office entrance, a school zone sign, and a military base gate. Documentary-style, showing the variety of restricted areas a traveler might encounter.]*

Even in the most firearm-friendly states in the country, certain locations are completely off-limits to armed individuals. Some of these restrictions are imposed by federal law and apply uniformly nationwide. Others are determined at the state level and can vary dramatically from one jurisdiction to the next. Knowing where you absolutely cannot carry — regardless of your permit status — is as important as knowing where you can.

**Federal Facilities: A Nationwide Prohibition**

The most consistent firearms restrictions in the United States are those imposed on federal property. These apply in every state, to every person, without exception.

Post offices — including their parking areas — are among the most commonly encountered federal no-carry zones. The prohibition extends to the building itself, the lobby, and any vehicle parked on postal service property. This restriction is enforced by the United States Postal Service and backed by federal criminal penalties.

Federal courthouses, administrative buildings, IRS offices, Social Security offices, and similar facilities are likewise off-limits. Any building owned or leased by the federal government where official business is conducted falls under this prohibition. Security screening at the entrance is common, and attempting to enter with a firearm — even inadvertently — can result in immediate arrest.

Properties managed by the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers present a unique challenge for travelers. The Corps oversees thousands of acres of land surrounding dams, reservoirs, and recreational waterways. Their "no firearms" policy extends to all land they own, manage, or lease — including campgrounds, boat ramps, and shoreline areas. This can create confusion at popular lakes where Corps-managed land borders state park land with different rules. A traveler who is legally carrying on a state park trail may unknowingly cross onto Corps property and suddenly be in violation of federal law.

Military installations impose their own comprehensive firearm restrictions. Visitors — whether military or civilian — generally may not bring firearms onto base. A recent Department of Defense directive provides limited exceptions for qualified personnel, but these exceptions are narrow and must be confirmed in advance. Travelers who live near or regularly pass through areas adjacent to military bases should familiarize themselves with the exact boundaries of those installations.

**Schools: The 1,000-Foot Rule**

Federal law creates a firearms exclusion zone extending 1,000 feet in every direction from the boundary of any K-12 school property. Within this zone, possessing a loaded firearm is a federal offense.

The exceptions to this rule are limited. Individuals who hold a concealed carry permit issued by the state where the school is located are generally exempt. Firearms that are unloaded and stored in locked containers within vehicles are exempt. Private property owners whose land falls within the zone are exempt on their own property.

What is not exempt: carrying under a permit issued by another state (even if honored through reciprocity), carrying under constitutional carry authority without a physical permit, or carrying a loaded firearm in a vehicle without meeting the locked-container requirement.

Given that K-12 schools are located throughout every urban and suburban area in the country, travelers should assume they will pass through multiple school zones during any drive through a populated area. Maintaining awareness of this restriction is a routine part of armed travel.

**State-Level Restrictions: The Expanding List**

Beyond federal prohibitions, each state maintains its own list of locations where firearms are banned or restricted. While the specifics are documented in the individual state profiles, the most common categories include:

**Courthouses and judicial facilities** — Nearly universal. Most states prohibit all firearms in any building where court proceedings take place, regardless of permit status.

**Law enforcement facilities and detention centers** — Jails, police stations, and sheriff's offices are typically off-limits. Some states extend this to the parking lots of these facilities as well.

**Polling places** — A growing number of states prohibit firearms at or near locations where voting takes place during elections. The buffer zone varies from the building itself to a specified distance around it.

**Legislative buildings** — State capitols and legislative office buildings generally prohibit firearms, though a handful of states make exceptions for permit holders or elected officials.

**Government offices** — Buildings where state or local government business is conducted — licensing offices, regulatory agencies, public meeting rooms — are frequently restricted.

**Mental health facilities and substance abuse treatment centers** — These locations are almost universally off-limits due to the vulnerability of the populations they serve.

**Day care and early childhood centers** — Separate from K-12 schools, these facilities are independently restricted in many states.

**Hotels and Lodging: Policy vs. Law**

No state in the country outright prohibits you from having a firearm in your hotel room. Several states — including some in the West and South — have enacted statutes that explicitly protect a guest's right to possess a loaded firearm in their lodging.

The complication arises from hotel policies. Many hotel chains have corporate rules prohibiting firearms on their premises. In most states, however, these policies do not carry the force of law. Violating a hotel's firearms policy is typically treated as a trespass issue: the hotel can ask you to leave, and if you refuse, you can be charged with trespassing. But merely having a firearm in your room is not a criminal act in most jurisdictions.

The exception occurs in states with "force of law" posting statutes or "informed consent" frameworks. In a small number of jurisdictions, a properly posted "no firearms" sign — or the absence of a sign explicitly allowing firearms — can make possession a criminal offense. These states are identified in the individual profiles.

**Restaurants That Serve Alcohol**

A common question among armed travelers is whether they can carry in restaurants that serve beer, wine, or cocktails alongside food. The answer depends on the state and on the type of establishment.

In most jurisdictions that allow restaurant carry, the law draws a distinction between dining establishments that happen to serve alcohol and bars or taverns where alcohol is the primary revenue source. Carrying in the former is frequently permitted for licensed individuals, provided they do not consume any alcohol themselves and remain in the dining area rather than the bar section. Carrying in the latter is typically prohibited.

This distinction matters because many popular chain restaurants — family dining establishments, steakhouses, casual eateries — hold liquor licenses and serve alcohol. In states that permit restaurant carry, you can dine at these locations while armed. In states that do not, entering with a firearm is a criminal offense.

**Houses of Worship**

Firearm policies at churches, mosques, synagogues, and temples vary by state and by individual institution. Some states allow carry in houses of worship by default, unless the institution has posted signage prohibiting it. Others prohibit carry unless the religious institution has affirmatively opted in by posting signage or passing a resolution allowing firearms.

A critical nuance: if the house of worship is located on the campus of a K-12 school — as many church buildings are — the school zone restriction may override any religious institution policy. This overlap has created legal uncertainty in several states and is an area where travelers should exercise particular caution.

**Highway Rest Areas**

Rest areas along interstate highways are isolated by nature. They are also among the locations where armed travelers are most likely to feel the need for a defensive firearm. Fortunately, most states allow lawful carry at rest areas, both inside buildings and on the surrounding grounds.

A few states restrict rest area carry to concealed carry only, even in states where open carry is generally permitted. Others limit carry to permit holders from that specific state, excluding travelers with out-of-state licenses. These details are noted in the state profiles.

In highly restrictive states, the McClure-Volkmer transport rules still apply to firearms stored properly in vehicles parked at rest areas. You may not be able to carry inside the building, but your firearm remains protected in your vehicle if it meets the federal transport requirements.

> ***Key Takeaway: Restricted areas exist everywhere — even in the most gun-friendly states. Federal facilities, school zones, courthouses, and government buildings are almost universally off-limits. Hotels, restaurants, and houses of worship present more nuanced situations that depend on state law and individual policy. Before entering any building or property, ask yourself whether firearms are permitted there — and if you are not certain, assume they are not.***
