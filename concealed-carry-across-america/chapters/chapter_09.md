**Chapter 9**

**Law Enforcement Officers and Preemption of Local Laws**

*[IMAGE: A retired law enforcement officer's desk showing a folded American flag, a framed badge, a concealed carry permit, and a holstered sidearm. Warm, respectful tone suggesting service and continued vigilance.]*

Two topics that frequently arise among traveling gun owners deserve their own dedicated treatment: the special carry privileges afforded to current and retired law enforcement officers, and the legal framework of preemption — the mechanism that determines whether firearm laws are uniform across a state or whether local governments can add their own restrictions.

**Active Duty Officers: Nationwide Carry Authority**

Federal law grants active duty law enforcement officers from any state, county, or municipal agency the authority to carry a concealed firearm while traveling anywhere in the United States. This privilege is not dependent on reciprocity agreements, state-issued permits, or destination-state licensing. It is a standalone federal right.

To exercise this authority, the officer must meet two conditions: they must carry official photographic identification issued by their employing agency, and they must be currently authorized to carry a firearm as part of their official duties. Off-duty officers who meet these criteria may carry in most public spaces, including locations that would otherwise be restricted for civilians.

There are, however, important limitations. The firearm must be concealed at all times — open carry under this federal authority is not permitted. Carry remains prohibited on certain federal properties, inside private establishments that have posted firearm prohibitions, and in any location where state law imposes restrictions that apply specifically to law enforcement personnel.

Officers should also be aware that state-level magazine capacity restrictions generally apply to them while traveling, though recent amendments to the federal statute exempt active duty officers from certain non-federal ammunition restrictions. This means an officer from a state with no magazine limits may need to swap out standard-capacity magazines for compliant ones when traveling through states with capacity restrictions — though enforcement of this requirement against active officers is uncommon.

**Retired Officers: Qualified Carry Under Federal Law**

Retired law enforcement officers enjoy travel carry privileges similar to those of active duty officers, but the qualifying criteria are more demanding.

To carry under the federal retired officer provision, an individual must have completed a minimum of ten years of aggregate service as a law enforcement officer with arrest authority and the responsibility to carry a firearm. Officers who separated from service earlier due to a service-connected disability may count probationary time toward this threshold.

In addition to meeting the service requirement, retired officers must maintain current firearms proficiency. This means passing an annual qualification course that meets the standards of their former employing state. The qualification can be administered by the former agency or by any certified firearms instructor in the retired officer's current state of residence.

Documentation requirements are strict. The retired officer must carry a photographic identification card issued by the agency from which they retired. This card must confirm their status as a retired law enforcement officer who is qualified to carry a firearm. Without this credential — even if the individual clearly served decades in law enforcement — the federal carry privilege does not apply.

**Why Officers Should Still Obtain a Civilian Permit**

Despite the federal carry authority available to both active and retired officers, experienced professionals strongly recommend maintaining a standard civilian concealed carry permit as well.

The reasoning is practical. Certain legal protections — including the exemption from the 1,000-foot school zone prohibition and the ability to carry in National Parks — are tied specifically to civilian permit holders. The federal law enforcement carry provisions may not explicitly cover these situations, creating potential gaps that a civilian permit can fill.

Additionally, during encounters with local law enforcement officers who may not be familiar with the federal statutes governing officer carry, producing a standard carry permit alongside agency credentials provides an additional layer of clarity. It establishes your legal status in a format that every officer immediately recognizes, reducing the likelihood of confusion or escalation.

**Preemption: Why Statewide Uniformity Matters**

For the average armed traveler, one of the most important — and most underappreciated — legal concepts is preemption. In the firearms context, preemption refers to a state law that prevents cities, counties, and other local governments from enacting their own firearms regulations. When preemption is in effect, the rules are the same whether you are in the state's largest city or its smallest rural township.

States with strong, comprehensive preemption laws are dramatically easier to travel through. You learn one set of rules and those rules apply everywhere within the state's borders. There is no risk of accidentally violating a municipal ordinance that exists nowhere else in the state. There is no need to research the specific firearms policies of every city and county on your route.

**The Patchwork Problem: States Without Preemption**

States that lack comprehensive preemption — or that allow significant exceptions — create a legal minefield for travelers. In these jurisdictions, a city council or county board may impose additional firearms restrictions that go beyond state law. These local ordinances might regulate where you can carry, how firearms must be stored in vehicles, whether open carry is permitted within city limits, or whether certain types of firearms or accessories are banned.

The practical effect is that a traveler driving through a single state might pass through a dozen different legal environments. Carrying a loaded handgun in the glove compartment might be legal on the rural highway but a misdemeanor offense within the limits of a city that has enacted its own storage requirements.

Some states occupy a middle ground: they have preemption statutes on the books but include carve-outs for certain categories — such as allowing cities to regulate open carry or to restrict firearms in local government buildings beyond what state law requires. Others have preemption laws that were on the books for years before being challenged and upheld — or struck down — by courts.

The state profiles in this guide note whether each jurisdiction has a preemption law, how comprehensive it is, and whether any significant local exceptions exist. Travelers should pay particular attention to this category when planning routes through states where they expect to pass through multiple cities and towns.

**Preemption with Enforcement Teeth**

A growing number of states have strengthened their preemption laws by adding penalty provisions for local officials who enact or enforce ordinances that violate state preemption. These penalties can include personal civil liability for the officials responsible, recovery of legal fees for citizens who successfully challenge illegal local ordinances, and in some cases, removal from office.

The addition of enforcement mechanisms has been a significant development, because preemption laws without penalties were often ignored by local governments that faced no consequences for passing firearms restrictions that conflicted with state law. Cities in several states openly maintained local firearms ordinances for years, knowing that the only recourse for affected citizens was expensive litigation.

Travelers benefit directly from strong preemption with enforcement provisions, because local governments in these states are far less likely to maintain rogue firearms restrictions that could trap an unsuspecting visitor.

> ***Key Takeaway: Active and retired law enforcement officers enjoy federal carry privileges that extend across state lines, but these rights come with documentation requirements and limitations. Both groups should maintain a civilian carry permit for the broadest possible legal coverage. For all armed travelers, preemption laws are a critical factor in trip planning — states with strong preemption offer consistent, predictable rules statewide, while states without preemption can present a dangerous patchwork of conflicting local ordinances. Check the preemption status of every state on your route before you leave.***
