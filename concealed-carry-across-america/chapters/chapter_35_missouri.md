**Chapter 35**

**Missouri**

*Firearm Friendliness Rating: 85/100 — A strongly pro-gun state with constitutional carry, universal permit recognition, strong preemption, and a frontier heritage that continues to shape its firearms laws.*

Missouri earned its reputation during the Civil War as a place where personal firearm ownership was essential for survival. That legacy endures as the state upholds strong Second Amendment rights through its adoption of constitutional carry, universal recognition of all out-of-state permits, an enhanced Castle Doctrine with stand-your-ground provisions, and a preemption law that generally prevents local governments from enacting their own firearms regulations.

**Quick-Reference Checklist**

- **Standard firearms & ammunition:** No license required for ownership or purchase
- **Semi-automatic firearms & standard-capacity magazines:** No restrictions on possession or sale
- **NFA items (machine guns, suppressors):** Lawful with proper federal compliance
- **Firearm law uniformity:** Strong preemption law; uniform regulations statewide, though localities may regulate the open carry of loaded weapons by non-permittees
- **Self-defense rights:** Enhanced Castle Doctrine enacted; stand-your-ground protections apply in public spaces
- **Open carry:** Lawful in most public areas, but local governments may restrict open carry by non-permittees
- **Concealed carry licensing:** Standard permits issued to residents only; five-year terms; extended-term and lifetime options available (valid within Missouri only)
- **Permitless carry:** Yes — any individual 19 or older (18 for military) who may lawfully possess a firearm
- **Out-of-state permit recognition:** All valid permits from every state are honored
- **Weapons allowed under permit:** Any lawful deadly weapon
- **Vehicle carry (non-permittees):** Loaded firearms may be carried anywhere in a vehicle
- **Vehicle carry at colleges:** Subject to college policy for permit holders
- **Vehicle carry at K-12 schools:** Missouri permit holders may carry a loaded handgun in their vehicle
- **Duty to notify law enforcement:** Required only upon direct request by an officer
- **Boondocking/RV carry:** Property owners may not prohibit firearms stored in vehicles on their premises
- **State parks:** Concealed handgun carry by permit holders and permitless carriers permitted
- **Restaurants serving alcohol:** Carry permitted while dining in food-service areas
- **Houses of worship:** Carry prohibited unless church leaders grant permission
- **Hotels:** Statutory allowance for guest rejection or ejection for firearm possession
- **Airports:** Carry permitted in non-secure terminal areas and parking facilities
- **Highway rest areas:** Carry permitted in buildings and on surrounding grounds

**For Permit Holders**

Missouri issues carry licenses to qualified residents who are at least 19 years old — or 18 if currently serving in the military. Standard permits are valid for five years and authorize the carry of any type of firearm. The state also offers extended-term and lifetime permits; however, these longer-term options are only valid within Missouri and do not qualify for recognition by other states.

Missouri does not issue permits to non-residents but recognizes valid carry permits from all other states. Both resident and recognized non-resident permit holders may carry either openly or concealed in most public places.

Firearms are prohibited in several specific locations including law enforcement offices, polling places, large sports arenas, K-12 schools, courthouses, childcare facilities, amusement parks, churches (unless permission is granted), government meetings and posted buildings, colleges, hospitals, casinos, bars, and private property with posted signage. These restrictions apply only to buildings — firearms secured in vehicles in parking lots remain lawful.

For individuals with a valid Missouri permit, a first-time violation of these location-based restrictions is not considered a criminal offense.

**Without a Permit**

Any U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident who is 19 or older (or 18 if in the military) may carry a loaded firearm anywhere inside a vehicle — including in the glove compartment, console box, or under the seat.

Concealed carry on foot is also permitted without a license, provided it does not occur in a prohibited location. However, unlike licensed carriers, individuals carrying without a permit can face criminal charges for violations. These penalties range from misdemeanors to felonies — most notably, carrying on K-12 school grounds without a permit is a felony offense. The only exception is carry on posted private property, which remains a non-criminal offense for non-permittees.

Open carry on foot is generally allowed in most public areas, but local governments may ban the open carry of loaded firearms by individuals who do not possess a permit. In such jurisdictions, local ordinances can make it unlawful for non-permittees to openly display a loaded firearm.

**Restrictions That Apply to Everyone**

Missouri's strong preemption law generally prohibits local governments from enacting their own gun regulations, ensuring uniformity across the state. Despite this, some localities — such as St. Louis — have attempted to impose restrictions, though such actions conflict with state law.

One important exception: firearms are prohibited on public buses. Regardless of permit status or constitutional carry rights, individuals are not allowed to possess firearms while riding or boarding a bus.

> ***Key Takeaway: Missouri is a highly favorable state for armed travelers. Constitutional carry, universal permit recognition, strong preemption, and vehicle protection laws create a welcoming environment. The key distinctions to understand are: (1) non-permittees face criminal penalties for carry violations that are treated as non-criminal offenses for permit holders; (2) carrying on K-12 school grounds without a permit is a felony; (3) local governments may restrict open carry by non-permittees; and (4) firearms are prohibited on public buses regardless of permit status. Obtaining a standard five-year permit remains valuable for the legal protections it provides and for interstate reciprocity.***
