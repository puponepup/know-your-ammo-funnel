## Concealed vs. Plain View
The definitions of "concealed" and "plain view" can vary by state, depending on statutes and case law. Generally, a concealed firearm is one that is readily accessible and hidden from ordinary observation on or about a person. In a vehicle, this typically includes firearms under outer clothing or in closed containers such as purses, gym bags, glove compartments, console boxes, or seat pockets—especially when within arm's reach.

Plain view refers to firearms that are clearly visible from outside the vehicle, typically positioned for immediate access while the vehicle is occupied. Examples include a holstered or slung firearm placed on the dashboard, passenger seat, or gun rack. States that permit plain view carry generally also allow firearms to be transported unloaded and cased in a trunk or rear storage area when the vehicle is unoccupied. Travelers are advised to secure firearms appropriately when not in the vehicle to comply with varying state requirements.

## Important Terms Defined

Standard firearms & ammo: Refers to commonly available, non-military-pattern handguns and long guns, along with standard commercial ammunition. Some states require a license to own or possess these items—even in one's own home. Where applicable, such licensing requirements are noted. Restrictions on exotic or uncommon items (e.g., flechette rounds, steel core armor-piercing, or explosive ammo) are not covered.

Semi-auto & high capacity magazines: Covers weapons with military-style features and magazines holding more than 10 rounds. Some states ban these based solely on appearance or capacity.

Machine guns & suppressors: Includes items regulated under the National Firearms Act, such as automatic weapons and silencers. Bump stocks, which simulate automatic fire, are also addressed where applicable.

Vehicle Carry for non-permittees: Most states allow permit holders to carry handguns anywhere in their vehicle. Non-permittees must follow specific rules, which are outlined per state. Any exceptions for permit holders are noted.

Posted: Refers to locations that display signage prohibiting firearms on the premises. Compliance is required where signs carry legal weight.

Permittee and non-permittee: A permittee holds a carry permit recognized by the state in question. A non-permittee does not. Unless stated otherwise, these terms refer to standard (non-enhanced) permits. Enhanced permits are only discussed when specifically relevant.

Securely encased: Firearms stored in closed, latched containers made for gun transport (e.g., hard plastic or metal gun cases). Items like purses, gym bags, or briefcases do not qualify as secure containers.

Constitutional Carry: Refers to states allowing some form of concealed carry without a permit. However, permitless carriers must still obey all restrictions on prohibited areas—just as permit holders do.

Weapons allowed for licensed carry: Most states only authorize handguns under a concealed carry permit. Some, such as Kentucky, permit the carry of any lawful deadly weapon.

State Parks: This guide covers the legality of handgun carry within legitimate state parks. Many states allow concealed carry in these areas while continuing to prohibit open carry and the possession of loaded long guns. Generally, if a state permits concealed carry, it also allows long guns to be transported unloaded and cased inside vehicles.

Restaurants Serving Alcohol: This section refers specifically to dining establishments, such as Applebee's or Fridays, that serve alcohol but primarily earn revenue from food sales. It does not include bars or taverns where alcohol is the main draw. If carry is allowed, it means licensed individuals may carry a concealed, loaded handgun only if they refrain from consuming alcohol, stay in the dining area, and avoid the bar section. Note: many states permit such establishments to post signage prohibiting firearms, which must be respected.

Houses of Worship: This includes churches, mosques, synagogues, and similar venues. In most states, these institutions may determine their own firearm policies. In some cases, carry is prohibited unless the institution explicitly allows it. In others, carry is permitted by default unless posted otherwise. A key caution: if the house of worship is located on otherwise restricted property (such as a K–12 school), it is considered off-limits unless exempted by state law.

Duty to Notify LEO of permit status: Some states require permit holders to immediately inform law enforcement officers upon first contact that they are armed and licensed. In others, disclosure is only required upon the officer's request. Failure to comply in immediate-notification states can lead to arrest, so travelers should be especially careful.

Red Flag Laws: Also known as Extreme Risk Protection Orders, these allow certain parties (e.g., police, family, coworkers) to petition a court to temporarily remove firearms from someone deemed a threat. While not directly targeting travelers, states with these laws are marked with a flag graphic in the upper-right margin of their entry. Most states that enforce red flag laws also tend to be more restrictive on other firearm issues.

Shall issue v. May Issue: Following the Bruen decision, discretionary or "may issue" permitting has been effectively eliminated. As a result, references to this distinction have been removed unless otherwise applicable.
