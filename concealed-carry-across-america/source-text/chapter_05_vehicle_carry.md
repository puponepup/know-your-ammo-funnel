## Vehicle Gun Possession at Colleges
While most states prohibit firearms inside K-12 school buildings, some allow exceptions for guns kept in vehicles parked on school property. This issue is addressed in the Traveler's Checklist section of the Guide, but readers should note several important limitations:

First, the exception applies only to individuals who hold a valid firearm license issued by the state where the school is located. This aligns with both federal and state law.

Under federal law, carrying a loaded firearm within 1,000 feet of a school is generally prohibited. However, there are exceptions for guns kept on private property and for those licensed by the state in which the school is located. For example, while a state like Kentucky may not require a permit for a loaded handgun in a vehicle, federal law still prohibits it unless the individual has a license from that specific state.

Second, the exemption covers only loaded handguns that are concealed from view and secured in a locked compartment when the vehicle is unattended. Openly visible firearms, even if not expressly prohibited by a state's law, are strongly discouraged. Furthermore, most states restrict carry privileges to handguns only—not long guns—whether loaded or not.

Finally, this guidance is primarily for non-student adult parents lawfully visiting campus events with a handgun in their vehicle. School employees may be subject to different rules. While they may not be violating criminal law in states that permit such possession, doing so could breach school personnel policies and impact their employment status.

## Loaded vs. Unloaded
The term "loaded" typically refers to a firearm that contains live ammunition in either the chamber or the magazine. In most states, a firearm is considered loaded if there are any rounds in the magazine, even if none are chambered. However, there are five notable exceptions—Colorado, Nevada, North Dakota, South Dakota, and Utah—where a firearm is only deemed loaded if there is a live round in the chamber.

Additionally, laws vary in how proximity to ammunition is treated. For example, New York considers a gun to be loaded if it is near a magazine containing live rounds. In contrast, some states prohibit magazines from being inserted into firearms but allow loaded magazines to be kept within arm's reach.

These state-by-state distinctions are documented throughout the Guide. Travelers should also take care to keep loaded firearms out of reach of children while in a vehicle. A small number of states criminalize storing firearms in locations where a child could potentially access them. Ohio and Colorado, for instance, have explicit statutes addressing this issue. Other states may pursue violations under broader child endangerment laws.
