## Open Carry
Open carry—carrying a handgun visibly on your person—is a lawful method of firearm possession in many states, particularly for individuals who do not have a recognized concealed carry permit. While this practice is not criminalized in numerous jurisdictions, travelers should exercise discretion and situational awareness, especially in urban or densely populated areas where open carry is uncommon.

Even in states where open carry is legally permitted, law enforcement may still stop and question individuals who openly display a firearm—such as one holstered on the hip. While no one should face harassment for exercising a fundamental legal right, real-world encounters with police scrutiny do happen.

To minimize complications, travelers should use good judgment and ensure that their handgun is secured in a clearly visible, snapped belt holster. They should also be aware that open carry is typically subject to the same location-based restrictions—such as bans in schools, government buildings, or private properties that prohibit firearms—as those that apply to concealed carry with a permit.

## Interstate Transport Through Restrictive States
Readers will find references throughout the Guide to the McClure-Volkmer Act of 1986, a federal law that amended the Gun Control Act of 1968. This legislation was intended to address and ease some of the overly restrictive provisions of the original law, particularly those concerning the interstate transport of firearms. Under McClure-Volkmer, individuals may legally travel through states where their firearms would otherwise be prohibited, provided specific conditions are met. Firearms must be unloaded, stored in a locked case, and placed in a trunk or other storage compartment that is not readily accessible to the vehicle's occupants. Ammunition must also be stored separately from the firearms. For vehicles without a trunk, a locked internal storage area—other than a glove box or center console—may be sufficient.

To qualify for this federal protection, the traveler must be passing through the restrictive state and headed to a destination where firearm possession is legal. Any extended stop beyond basic necessities like fuel or emergency services may invalidate this protection and subject the traveler to the firearm laws of the state they are in. For example, someone driving from Kentucky to Maine with lawfully owned Class III machine guns will pass through New York, where such weapons are illegal. As long as the firearms are properly stored and the traveler makes no extended stops, they remain protected under federal law. However, stopping to visit relatives in Albany would place the traveler under New York's jurisdiction and could result in arrest for illegal firearm possession.

Some travelers have found it beneficial to carry hotel or campground pre-registration forms as evidence of their intended final destination. This can help demonstrate to law enforcement that they are simply transiting through a restrictive state and not planning to remain there. It's also important to note that the McClure-Volkmer Act explicitly covers only firearms and ammunition. Items such as high-capacity magazines or other restricted firearm components may not be protected under the statute, and the courts have yet to fully address these gray areas.

## Traffic Stops
Any traveler carrying firearms should be well-informed on how to properly handle a routine traffic stop. Frequent travelers will inevitably be pulled over at some point for a minor infraction such as speeding. During a stop, motorists should remain calm and avoid sudden or erratic movements. Both hands should be kept visibly on the steering wheel while the driver stays seated, waiting for the officer to approach. Once contact is made, the officer has the right to ask questions regarding the vehicle's operation. If a question may lead to self-incriminating responses, the traveler has the right to politely decline to answer and state that they would prefer to consult legal counsel before responding.

In some cases, an officer may wish to search the vehicle for contraband, including firearms—especially in states with stricter gun laws. To perform a lawful search, the officer must generally have probable cause. However, since most routine traffic stops do not meet this threshold, officers may instead request voluntary consent to search the vehicle. Many people mistakenly comply out of fear that refusal will appear suspicious. This is not advisable. Giving consent waives important legal protections and allows the officer to conduct a broad search without further justification. Travelers are advised to politely refuse such requests and let the officer know they do not consent to a search. In most cases, if the officer lacks probable cause, they will not proceed without a signed consent form.

Travelers should also be aware that officers are permitted to conduct a limited protective search, or "frisk," of a person during a traffic stop if they have reasonable suspicion that the individual may be armed and dangerous. This may include asking the person to exit the vehicle and checking their clothing for concealed weapons, as well as searching the immediate area around the driver's seat. However, this type of search cannot legally extend to the rest of the vehicle unless the officer can articulate probable cause of criminal activity.

A common question among permit holders is whether they must proactively inform an officer that they are carrying a firearm. The answer depends on the state. While most states do not require a permittee to volunteer this information unless asked, some states mandate immediate disclosure during an interaction with law enforcement. These states are noted elsewhere in the Guide. As a general rule of safety and transparency, it is recommended that recognized permit holders inform the officer that they are carrying a firearm and have a valid permit. This can help de-escalate tension and avoid misunderstandings. Note that this applies to firearms being carried under the authority of a permit. Guns that are unloaded, cased, and stored in a trunk or locked compartment typically do not need to be disclosed unless the officer initiates a search.

Finally, travelers should be aware of emerging tactics used by law enforcement in certain anti-gun jurisdictions. Reports have surfaced—particularly in states like Maryland—of police using license plate reader cameras to identify and stop out-of-state gun owners. In one case, a Florida family was detained, searched, and held for nearly two hours because a Maryland trooper accessed information indicating the driver had a Florida concealed carry permit. The trooper used this as a pretext to conduct an invasive search and temporarily detain the family, despite no other evidence of wrongdoing. While it is unclear whether this represents official policy, the incident highlights that law enforcement in some states may know your permit status before initiating a stop. Travelers should keep this in mind when passing through states with strict firearm laws.
