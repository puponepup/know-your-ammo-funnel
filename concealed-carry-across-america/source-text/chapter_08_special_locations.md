## Hotels & Lodgings
Questions often arise regarding the legality of carrying firearms in hotels. No state outright prohibits firearm carry in hotels, and in fact, states like Montana and Texas specifically protect the right of travelers to possess loaded firearms in their hotel rooms. However, in most states, the issue is left to the individual policies of the hotel. This distinction is important. While a hotel may have a policy against firearm possession, that policy often carries no force of law. In such cases, the hotel can ask a guest to leave if they are found carrying a gun, but unless the guest refuses to comply, no criminal penalties apply.

The Traveler's Checklist in the Guide addresses this nuance. If a hotel is located in a state where a properly posted "no firearms" policy can result in criminal charges, this is noted in the entry for that state with the phrase "force of law." Likewise, if a state has statutes allowing a hotel to reject or eject a guest for firearm possession, that is also clearly indicated. In states where there are no applicable laws on the matter, the situation is simply classified as "subject to policy enforcement."

Travelers should also be aware of a newer legal framework known as "informed consent" laws. In states like Hawaii, New York, and California, private businesses are presumed to prohibit firearm carry unless they explicitly post signage allowing it. This approach reverses the more common rule and requires businesses to opt in to allow carry, rather than opt out.

The guidance in this section assumes the traveler is in legal possession of their firearm, ideally with a recognized concealed carry permit, and is not engaged in any criminal activity. While it is not recommended to violate a hotel's policy—even if it lacks legal enforcement—there may be situations where personal safety or circumstance requires making a judgment call. This section is designed to provide travelers with the legal context necessary to make an informed decision.

## Highway Rest Areas
Rest areas, often remote and isolated, are locations where lawful defensive carry is especially important due to increased risk of crime. Most states allow firearm carry at rest areas, but a few prohibit it either in buildings or on the grounds. These details are outlined in the Traveler's Checklist, assuming the traveler is licensed to carry in that state. For instance, Rhode Island permits carry, but only with a Rhode Island-issued permit.

Some states may require firearms to remain concealed at rest areas, even if open carry is normally allowed. In highly restrictive states, protections under the McClure-Volkmer Act still apply if the firearm remains unloaded and secured in the vehicle. Travelers may also encounter unauthorized "no guns" signs, which are rare and generally unenforceable.

## National Parks & Forests
Firearm possession in National Parks and wildlife refuges is governed by 16 USC section 1a-7b, which defers to the gun laws of the state where the park or refuge is located. If your permit is valid in that state, you may carry in outdoor areas like trails and campsites, subject to local carry restrictions. However, official buildings such as visitor centers and ranger stations remain off-limits to all firearms. Private businesses within the parks, such as gift shops or restaurants, are not automatically restricted but may post signs to prohibit carry.

National Forests, managed by the Department of Agriculture, follow state law as well. If open carry is allowed in the state, it's generally allowed in the National Forest—unless signage or local laws say otherwise. Travelers may encounter "no firearms" signs in forests located in otherwise gun-friendly states. These may reflect either actual legal restrictions or unofficial enforcement attempts, so it's wise to be cautious, as officials may enforce such rules regardless of their legal standing.

## Indian Reservations
Indian reservations often have stricter firearm carry rules than the states in which they are located, due to their status as quasi-sovereign nations. While this Guide does not cover every reservation individually, general vehicle carry guidelines for several major reservations are provided. For example, on many reservations in Arizona (Navajo, Hopi, Yavapai), loaded firearms are permitted in the glove compartment, luggage, or trunk. In Colorado, the Southern Ute allow loaded handguns in vehicles but require long guns to be chamber-unloaded. Florida generally applies state firearm laws to its reservations. In Idaho, carry rules vary—Nez Perce allows loaded guns in plain view or concealed with any state permit, while Fort Hall permits carry if the firearm is stowed. Minnesota reservations like Red Lake and White Earth require all firearms to be unloaded, cased, and stowed. In Montana, loaded carry is typically allowed in the trunk or glove compartment, though Fort Peck requires a permit unless the gun is in plain view.

Other areas have stricter rules: Pyramid Lake (Nevada) prohibits all gun possession, while Walker River requires firearms to be unloaded and cased. Some reservations, such as Cherokee Nation in Oklahoma, allow permitless and permitted carry, while others—like the Menominee in Wisconsin—require a tribal-issued permit for loaded carry. Standing Rock (ND/SD), Warm Springs (Oregon), and Colville (Washington) require guns to be unloaded, cased, and stowed in the trunk or rear area. In Utah, the Uintah & Ouray reservation allows loaded handguns anywhere in the vehicle, but long guns must be unloaded. Wind River (Wyoming) allows loaded firearms in plain view or the trunk unless the traveler holds a valid permit.

Travelers should exercise caution when passing through reservations. Carrying firearms on foot is strongly discouraged, and unless otherwise listed, all firearms should be transported unloaded, cased, and secured in accordance with the McClure-Volkmer Act.
