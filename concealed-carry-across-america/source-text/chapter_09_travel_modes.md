## Motorcycle Issues
Motorcycle riders often wonder how firearm carry laws—typically written for four-wheel vehicles—apply to them. Questions frequently arise about whether a saddlebag or rear compartment qualifies as a legal storage area for transporting a firearm. While most state laws do not specifically address motorcycles, riders can generally follow a few practical guidelines to remain compliant.

A motorcycle's rear compartment, if it is lockable, is typically treated the same as a vehicle trunk. Storing an unloaded firearm in this locked space is usually acceptable in states that prohibit loaded firearms inside vehicles. In states that recognize your concealed carry permit, carrying a loaded firearm on your person—such as in a holster—is legal. Similarly, a tote bag or tank bag on the motorcycle functions like a briefcase or gym bag in a car and may be interpreted as concealed carry, requiring a permit in most jurisdictions.

For riders without a permit in open carry states, wearing a handgun in a hip holster in plain view is often legal. However, this approach may draw unwanted attention from other motorists or law enforcement, even if lawful. Riders must weigh the benefit of being armed with the risk of increased scrutiny during travel.

## Motorhome and RV Issues
A common question among RV owners is whether their motorhomes are considered vehicles or residences. Most states treat RVs as vehicles while in motion, meaning firearm laws related to vehicular travel apply. However, courts have ruled that once an RV is in a fixed location—such as a designated campground and connected to utilities—it may be classified as a residence. This distinction matters, as many states allow loaded firearm possession in a home but prohibit it in a vehicle. RV owners should stay aware of their RV's legal classification based on its current state.

Another frequent concern is the issue of "trunk transport". RVs typically lack a traditional trunk, but owners should treat external compartments or any locked internal compartment as the legal equivalent. The key legal standard is that the compartment must require exiting the vehicle to access—just like a car trunk. This same logic applies to trailers, which are also considered separate from the passenger area.

Full-size RVs pose a challenge for plain view carry, especially when the cab is elevated. If a firearm cannot be seen from outside the vehicle, it doesn't qualify as being in plain view. In such cases, owners should consider using console boxes or glove compartments, which are legal in many states for non-permit holders and do not require visibility.

The growing practice of "boondocking"—camping in non-designated areas like parking lots—raises another legal issue. What if the property has a "no guns" policy, even for parked vehicles? Some states have "parking lot protection" laws that prevent property owners from banning firearms in parking areas, provided the weapon stays concealed in the vehicle. The Guide's Traveler's Checklist details which states offer such protections.

## Traveling by Air, Cruise Ship, Train & Greyhound Bus
Federal law prohibits firearms in the sterile areas of airports (i.e., beyond security screening), but travelers may transport unloaded firearms in checked baggage. Firearms must be secured in a locked, hard-sided case, and ammunition must be stored in factory boxes, not loose or loaded in magazines. Most airlines allow firearms and ammunition to be packed in the same case unless their internal policy states otherwise. At check-in, passengers must declare the firearm and provide a key or combination if TSA requests to inspect the case. Since procedures vary by airline, it's wise to call ahead to confirm where to check in and what forms are needed. Federal law prohibits airlines from marking luggage as containing a firearm—any visible labeling would be illegal.

Travelers who are not flying may be allowed to carry in airport parking lots and non-sterile terminal areas, depending on state law. Some states prohibit firearms on all airport property, while others leave the decision to local authorities.

Importantly, a recent federal court ruling clarified that the McClure-Volkmer Act protects vehicular transport only, not air travel. This means travelers with layovers in restrictive states could face legal trouble if their firearm—though legal at the origin and destination—is illegal in the connecting state.

When traveling by train, bus, cruise ship, or other common carriers, passengers must typically surrender their firearms and ammunition to the captain, conductor, or designated official for the duration of the trip. Policies vary widely, so travelers should always contact the carrier in advance to confirm specific procedures and requirements.

## Over the Road Commercial Trucks
One persistent rumor in the trucking industry is that federal law prohibits firearms in commercial vehicles, such as semi-tractor trailers. This claim has been repeated by supervisors and corporate management for years—often without any legal citation to support it. In reality, no federal law prohibits truckers from carrying firearms. Many companies have internal policies banning firearms in their vehicles, but these are corporate rules, not federal statutes. Truckers are subject to the same state and local firearm laws as any other traveler. Independent operators typically have more flexibility, since they aren't bound by employer policies. Regardless of company policy, a trucker who carries a firearm for personal protection is not violating any federal law.
