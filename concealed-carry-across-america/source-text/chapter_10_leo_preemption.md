## Current & Retired Law Enforcement Officers
Federal law permits active duty law enforcement officers from any state or local agency to carry concealed firearms while traveling. To do so, the officer must possess official photo identification issued by their department and be authorized to carry a firearm by that agency. This right extends to most public areas, but the firearm must remain concealed, and carry is still prohibited on certain government properties and private premises where firearms are restricted. Officers must also comply with state magazine capacity limits, though recent amendments exempt them from non-federal ammunition restrictions.

Retired law enforcement officers are granted similar travel carry rights, though the criteria are more stringent. A retired officer must have completed at least 10 years of service with authority to carry a firearm and conduct arrests and investigations. If an officer left earlier due to a service-connected disability, probationary service may be counted. Retired officers must annually meet their state's firearm qualification standards—either through their former agency or a certified instructor in their current state. They must also carry photographic ID issued by the agency from which they retired, confirming their law enforcement status.

Lastly, both active and retired officers are strongly encouraged to maintain a valid civilian concealed carry permit. Certain federal protections—such as those for carry in National Parks or within school zones—may only apply to civilian permit holders. While professional courtesy often plays a role, having a permit can help avoid confusion or legal complications during interactions with local authorities.

## Preemption of Local Laws
Firearm preemption laws prevent local governments—such as cities and counties—from creating their own regulations on firearm possession, ownership, or carry. In many states, these laws establish full uniformity by prohibiting any form of home rule, while others allow grandfathered local ordinances or grant limited local authority in specific areas, such as open carry. These distinctions are outlined in the Traveler's Checklist for each state, helping travelers understand whether certain issues—like carrying openly or storing a firearm in the glove compartment—are subject to local variation. Planning travel is significantly easier in states with comprehensive preemption laws, as firearm regulations remain consistent statewide.
